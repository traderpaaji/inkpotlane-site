Einstein's Riddle: Did Einstein Write It? Plus a Mini Version

Einstein's riddle: the earliest known printing of this zebra puzzle is a 1962 magazine. Check the Einstein attribution, then solve a smaller version.

Inkpot Lane · 11 November 2026 · 8 min read

Five houses stand in a row, each a different color. Their residents have different nationalities, pets, drinks and cigarette brands, and fifteen numbered statements give you enough information to work out who lives where. Some versions of the puzzle come with an extra challenge attached: a claim that Albert Einstein wrote it as a boy, and that only a tiny percentage of people can solve it. Einstein's riddle is interesting for two reasons. The logic puzzle has a definite answer; the story about its famous author has no supporting evidence.

The short answer: Einstein's riddle is the puzzle better known to puzzlers as the Zebra Puzzle. Its earliest known version appeared in Life International magazine on 17 December 1962. It is often said that Einstein invented it as a boy, and sometimes that Lewis Carroll did, but there is no evidence for either. It is a logic grid puzzle with five houses and fifteen numbered statements, asking the reader to identify the water drinker and the zebra owner.

The case file: what the record shows

Here is what can actually be documented, from Wikipedia's article on the Zebra Puzzle and the sources it cites:

  • 17 December 1962. The puzzle appears in Life International. It describes five houses and gives fifteen numbered statements, ending with a challenge to identify the water drinker and the zebra owner.
  • 25 March 1963. Life publishes the solution, together with the names of several hundred successful solvers from around the world.
  • Later. The puzzle is retold many times, with the colors, nationalities, drinks and pets swapped for others, but with the same logic underneath. One popular variant asks who owns the fish.

The legend: three claims, checked

"Einstein wrote it as a boy." The Wikipedia article describes this as an urban legend and says there is no evidence for it. Einstein was born in 1879, so a puzzle by him as a boy would date to the 1880s or 1890s. The earliest known printing dates from 1962, seven years after his death in 1955. That date does not rule out earlier authorship by itself, but no evidence has been found connecting Einstein to the puzzle. (The 1879 and 1955 dates are on Wikipedia's Albert Einstein page.)

"Lewis Carroll wrote it." The same article notes that the puzzle is sometimes attributed to Carroll, and again that there is no evidence for his authorship.

"Only 2 percent of people can solve it." We could find no source for this claim (no test, no defined sample, no measurement), and the Wikipedia article does not mention it. Life printed the names of several hundred successful solvers in March 1963, but without knowing how many people tried, that number says nothing about the percentage who could solve it. The claim is unsupported, not disproved.

So the fairest description is the dull one. The puzzle's author is unknown, its first known appearance is a magazine in December 1962, and the famous names were attached later.

Why the Einstein story stuck

There is no documented answer to that. One possible explanation is that a famous genius's name makes the puzzle sound more important, the story that he invented it as a boy adds a challenge, and the supposed small success rate makes solving it feel exclusive. That is our interpretation of the legend, not a documented history of how it spread. None of those claims helps you solve it. What helps is the method, and the method is the same one used for every logic grid puzzle.

How Einstein's riddle actually works

The puzzle is a grid in disguise. Every clue is one of a few types:

  • Same house: one fact goes with another (in our mini version below: "The dog lives in the red house").
  • Exact position: a fact is pinned to one house ("The tea drinker lives in the middle house").
  • Next to: two facts sit in neighboring houses ("The milk drinker lives next door to the cat").
  • Somewhere to the right: one house is farther right than another, with or without houses between them (in our mini version, the green house is somewhere to the right of the red house).
  • Immediately to the right: one house stands directly beside another, on its right, with no house between them. The original five-house puzzle uses this stricter kind of clue.

You set up a table with one column per house and one row per category, fill in the fixed positions first, then use the next-to and right-of clues to rule options out. When a category has only one place left for an item, it goes there. The original five-house puzzle is reproduced in Wikipedia's article on the Zebra Puzzle. If you want to attempt it without seeing the answer first, try it there before reading its solution; the answer is also in the questions at the end of this article. Try a smaller one: three houses

Here is an original three-house version, written for this article, so you can feel how the puzzle works in a few minutes. We checked it by computer: it has exactly one answer, and every clue is needed.

Three houses stand in a row: left, middle and right. Each is painted a different color (red, blue or green), each has a different pet (a cat, a dog or a fish), and in each the owner drinks something different (tea, milk or juice).

  1. The green house is somewhere to the right of the red house.
  2. The tea drinker lives in the middle house.
  3. The dog lives in the red house.
  4. The milk drinker lives next door to the cat.
  5. The blue house is at one end of the row.
  6. The juice drinker does not live in the blue house.

Who keeps the fish, and what do they drink?

The solution, step by step

  • Clues 1 and 5 together. Blue is at one end. If blue were on the left, red and green would fill the middle and right, with red left of green, so red would be in the middle. If blue were on the right, red would be on the left and green in the middle. Two possibilities.
  • Clue 2. Tea is in the middle in both cases.
  • Test blue on the left. Juice is not in the blue house (clue 6) and tea is in the middle, so juice is on the right and milk on the left. The dog is in the red house, which is the middle (clue 3). Milk is next to the cat (clue 4), and the only house next to the left one is the middle, but the dog is there. Contradiction: blue cannot be on the left.
  • So: red on the left, green in the middle, blue on the right. Tea in the middle; juice is not in blue, so juice is on the left and milk on the right. The dog is in the red house, on the left. Milk (right) is next to the cat, so the cat is in the middle. The fish is in the blue house.

Answer: the fish lives in the blue house on the right, and its owner drinks milk.

Notice that you never guessed. You split the puzzle into two cases, followed one until it broke, and the other was left standing. That is the whole method of Einstein's riddle, at a larger size.

Frequently asked questions

Did Einstein really write Einstein's riddle?

There is no evidence that he did. The earliest known version appeared in Life International in December 1962, years after his death, and the attribution to Einstein is described as an urban legend.

What is the answer to Einstein's riddle?

In the original Zebra Puzzle, the Norwegian (in the yellow house, first in the row) drinks water, and the Japanese man (in the green house, fifth in the row) owns the zebra.

Is Einstein's riddle the same as the zebra puzzle?

Yes. "Zebra Puzzle" is the name used by puzzlers; "Einstein's riddle" and "Einstein's puzzle" are popular names for the same puzzle and its variants. Versions that ask "who owns the fish?" use different details with the same logic.

Is Einstein's riddle hard?

It takes patience rather than special knowledge: no trick, just careful elimination with a grid. Life's 1963 solution named several hundred people who had solved it.

What kind of puzzle is Einstein's riddle?

A logic grid puzzle: a set of people or houses, several categories of facts, and clues that let you work out which fact belongs to which house by elimination.
