Christmas Eve Traditions for Kids: 5 Ideas and Their Stories

Five Christmas Eve traditions for kids and their stories: stockings, Santa's snack, the Santa tracker, Iceland's book gifts, and one quiet craft.

Inkpot Lane · 10 November 2026 · 8 min read

Christmas Eve is the longest evening of a child's year. The tree is lit, the presents are wrapped, and there are still four hours until bedtime and about four hundred questions to answer. A few Christmas Eve traditions for kids can give that long evening a shape: something to prepare, something to enjoy together, and a familiar ending at the bedroom door. Some are much older than they look, others are twentieth-century inventions, and their stories can be part of the fun.

The short answer: Five Christmas Eve traditions for kids: hang the stockings and read the poem first printed in 1823; leave Santa a snack and the reindeer a carrot; check the Santa tracker, which began in 1955; give a book to read that night, borrowed from Iceland's Christmas book culture; and make one quiet thing together. Each has a different story. Pick the two or three that suit your children's ages, not all five.

Christmas Eve traditions for kids, and how old they really are

The traditions below come from different places and periods. Some grew slowly; others have a recorded moment that made them popular. Either way, the good news is the same: a family can start a tradition this year, and it will be just as real by the time your children have children.

1. Hang the stockings, then read the poem that made them famous

On 23 December 1823 a newspaper in Troy, New York, printed a long, unsigned poem called "Account of a Visit from St. Nicholas." It is the poem most people know as "'Twas the Night Before Christmas," and it is set on exactly this evening. Its third line is the one that matters here:

The stockings were hung by the chimney with care,

Stockings were part of St. Nicholas customs before the poem: in 1810 the New-York Historical Society handed out a printed picture of St. Nicholas, commissioned by John Pintard, that showed two stockings hanging beside a fireplace. What the 1823 poem did was carry that scene to a much wider audience: the stockings hung at the fireplace before bed, and St. Nicholas filling them in the night. Reading the poem after the stockings go up turns a chore into a scene. Children can check the room against the poem: chimney, stockings, everyone in bed.

The poem takes about five minutes to read aloud. If you want it in full, with the story of who wrote it and some tips for reading it to small children, the night before Christmas poem is here.

Best for: sharing across ages. Younger children can join in with the reindeer names; older children may enjoy learning that the last two were first printed as "Dunder and Blixem."

2. Leave a snack for Santa, and something for the reindeer

Milk and cookies by the fireplace feels as old as Christmas, but nobody can trace it with certainty. History.com's account of the custom says it "took off as an American holiday tradition in the 1930s, during the Great Depression," and mentions one theory that links it, much further back, to stories of children leaving food for the horse of the Norse god Odin. Treat that second part as a theory, not a fact; the honest answer is that the origin is unclear.

There is a related custom in the Netherlands and Belgium, where children leave a carrot or some hay in their shoes for St. Nicholas's horse; it belongs to the St. Nicholas celebrations of 5 and 6 December rather than to Christmas Eve, but it is the same idea. Families can borrow the idea for Santa's reindeer: a carrot beside the cookies gives a small child a job, and gives you a reason to leave it with a bite out of it in the morning.

Best for: ages 2 to 8. Let the children choose the plate, pour the milk and write a short note.

3. Track Santa across the sky

This one began with a wrong number. On 30 November 1955, a child trying to telephone Santa reached the Continental Air Defense Command's operations center in Colorado Springs instead, and Colonel Harry Shoup answered. Whether a Sears newspaper advertisement printed the number wrongly or the child misdialed it is still disputed. The command played along, and the tradition has run every year since, continued by its successor, NORAD, the North American Aerospace Defense Command.

Today the NORAD Tracks Santa website opens on 1 December, and the tracking itself starts on 24 December. For children old enough to read a map, it turns the evening into a small geography lesson: where is he now, and how far is that from us?

Best for: ages 5 and up. For younger children, check it once before bed rather than watching all evening.

4. A book on Christmas Eve

Iceland's Christmas book culture has a lovely name: Jólabókaflóð, the Christmas book flood. Strictly, the phrase describes the yearly rush of new books published before Christmas. Giving books as Christmas presents, including on Christmas Eve, is an established Icelandic custom. During the Second World War, limits on imported goods helped make locally printed books an appealing gift, and the yearly catalog of new books, the Bókatíðindi, began in 1944.

The picture many people share online, everyone reading in bed with hot chocolate all night, is, as Wikipedia notes, a romanticized one. You do not need the romantic version. One new book, opened on Christmas Eve and read together before lights out, is enough.

Best for: every age. For non-readers, a picture book or a coloring book with a story in it; for older children, a chapter book they are allowed to read in bed.

5. One quiet thing to make

If the hours between dinner and bedtime feel long, set out something simple to make together at the kitchen table: a card for someone visiting on Christmas Day, a paper star for the window, or a coloring page. It does not need a history. It becomes one the moment you do it two years running.

How to put an evening together

You will not do all five, and you should not try. A good Christmas Eve for small children has a shape: something active early, something calm late, and the same ending every year.

  • Ages 2 to 4. Snack for Santa and a carrot for the reindeer before dinner; the poem, or just the first page of it, at bedtime. Keep it short. Their evening ends early.
  • Ages 5 to 8. One check on the Santa tracker after dinner, a coloring page or a card at the table, stockings up, then the whole poem. Keeping the same order each year makes the evening easy to follow.
  • Ages 9 and up. Give them a role: they read the poem to the younger ones, they pour Santa's milk, they are in charge of the tracker. A book of their own to open on Christmas Eve keeps the evening theirs as well.

Whatever you choose, end the same way every year. The cookies may change, the books may change, and the activities may change; the evening can still have one familiar ending, the same poem read together, year after year.

Frequently asked questions

What are good Christmas Eve traditions for young kids?

Short, hands-on ones that end at bedtime: leaving a snack for Santa and a carrot for the reindeer, hanging stockings, and reading "'Twas the Night Before Christmas" in bed. For toddlers, one or two of these is plenty.

When did leaving milk and cookies for Santa start?

Nobody knows for certain. It became a popular American tradition in the 1930s, according to History.com; older roots are sometimes suggested, but they are theories rather than documented history.

How did NORAD start tracking Santa?

In 1955 a child trying to call Santa reached a military operations center in Colorado Springs; whether the number was misprinted in an advertisement or misdialed is disputed. Colonel Harry Shoup answered, the command played along, and its successor NORAD still tracks Santa every Christmas Eve.

What is the Icelandic Christmas Eve book tradition?

In Iceland books are a favorite Christmas present and are given on Christmas Eve. Jólabókaflóð, the Christmas book flood, strictly means the yearly rush of new books before Christmas; the national catalog of new books began in 1944.
