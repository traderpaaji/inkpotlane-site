What Is a Whodunit? The 1930 Word and the Shape of the Story

What is a whodunit? A mystery built on one question, who did it, with the clues shared so you can answer first. Where the 1930 word came from, and its shape.

Inkpot Lane · 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

In 1930, Donald Gordon used the word "whodunit" in a News of Books review of Milward Kennedy's Half-Mast Murder, according to Wikipedia's article on the word, which cites Merriam-Webster. The question behind the word is simple: who has done it? So what is a whodunit? It is a mystery built around identifying the culprit. In a fair-play whodunit, the writer gives the reader the clues needed to try to reach the answer before the detective explains it.

The short answer: A whodunit is a plot-driven kind of detective story in which the identity of the culprit is the central puzzle. The word is a slangy squeeze of "who has done it?", first recorded in 1930. A good whodunit shows you the suspects and the clues, then pauses before the reveal so you can name the culprit yourself.

Where the word came from

The 1930 credit is not the only claim. Wikipedia's article records that the journalist Wolfe Kaufman later said he coined the word around 1935 while working at the show-business paper Variety, and that a later Variety editor, Abel Green, credited the paper's founder, Sime Silverman. The earliest Variety use the article cites is a headline in the edition of 28 August 1934. Of the three, the 1930 review is the earliest use on record.

The spelling has never fully settled either. "Whodunit" is the standard form; "whodunnit", with a double n, is a common alternative, and you will see both on book covers.

What is a whodunit, as a kind of story?

At the center of a whodunit is a question of identity: who committed the crime? How the crime was done may matter, and there may be a chase or danger, but identifying the culprit is what drives the plot. Several people could have done it. One did. The reader gets the chance to work out which one.

That is why whodunits tend to share a shape, whoever writes them:

  1. A small world. A village, a house party, a train, a harbor town: a closed circle of people who can all be suspected.
  2. The puzzle. Something is stolen, broken, hidden or worse, and it matters to someone.
  3. The suspects. Each has a reason, a chance, or a story that does not quite fit.
  4. The clues. Small facts, often ordinary ones, laid out where the reader can see them.
  5. The pause and the reveal. The detective explains, and every step of the explanation uses something you were shown.

That fifth step is especially important to a fair-play whodunit. Thrillers more often build suspense around danger and what happens next; whodunits put more weight on identifying the culprit. Some mysteries do both.

The golden age of the whodunit

The form flourished between the two world wars: Wikipedia's article on the Golden Age of Detective Fiction places that period mainly in the 1920s and 1930s. Some writers of that period treated the whodunit as a game between author and reader, and wrote down its rules.

In 1929 the writer Ronald Knox published his "Ten Rules for a Good Detective Story" (the cited source is Publishers Weekly, 5 October 1929). Among them, in paraphrase: the culprit should appear early in the story; no supernatural explanations; no more than one secret room or passage; and the detective must not turn out to be the culprit. A year before that, in September 1928, the American writer S. S. Van Dine had published his "Twenty Rules for Writing Detective Stories" in The American Magazine. And in 1930, the same year the word "whodunit" appeared in print, a group of British writers formed the Detection Club, whose members, according to the Golden Age article, agreed to follow Knox's rules.

The details of those rules have aged unevenly, and writers have broken most of them on purpose since. The idea underneath them has lasted: the reader should be given a fair chance.

A fair chance: what "fair play" means in practice

A whodunit plays fair when everything the detective uses to reach the answer is shown to the reader first. That rules out three common disappointments:

  • The late surprise. A clue that only appears in the final explanation.
  • The stranger at the door. A culprit who was never on the page until the reveal.
  • The hidden thought. A detective who knows something the reader was never told.

Fair play does not mean easy. The clue may be a remark about a clock, a pair of boots on the wrong feet, or a bell that should have rung and did not. It is in plain sight; you just have to notice that it matters. Our article on short mysteries to solve has an original one to try, with the solution set out clue by clue.

Whodunit, mystery, detective story: is there a difference?

The words overlap, and nobody polices them, but they lean in different directions:

WordWhat the story asksExample of the question
MysteryWhat happened?Where did the money go?
Detective storyHow will the detective solve it?What will the detective notice?
WhodunitWhich of these people did it?Was it the baker, the tuner or the clerk?
ThrillerWill the hero survive or stop it in time?Can they get there before midnight?

Many books are more than one of these at once. A whodunit is the one that puts the identity question at the center and lets you answer it.

How to read a whodunit so you can solve it

  • Name the suspects early. When the circle of people is drawn, list them, even in your head.
  • Notice the dull details. Times, weather, what someone was carrying, what was missing from a room. Whodunit clues often hide in ordinary sentences.
  • Stop before the reveal. If the book gives you a pause, take it. Say a name out loud before you turn the page.
  • Check your reasoning, not just your guess. A lucky guess is fun; seeing which clue you missed is how you get better.

Frequently asked questions

What is a whodunit in simple words?

A story where a crime or puzzle has happened, several people could be responsible, and the fun is working out which one did it before the detective tells you.

Is it spelled whodunit or whodunnit?

Both are used. "Whodunit" is the standard spelling; "whodunnit" is a common alternative.

Who invented the word whodunit?

The credit usually goes to Donald Gordon, who used it in a 1930 review in News of Books. Two later claims connected with the newspaper Variety also exist, but the 1930 use is the earliest one on record.

What is the difference between a whodunit and a mystery?

Every whodunit is a mystery, but not every mystery is a whodunit. A whodunit puts the question "which of these people did it?" at the center and gives the reader the clues to answer it.

Does a whodunit have to involve a murder?

No. Many whodunits do, but the form only needs a puzzle of identity: a theft, a disappearance, a hoax. A cozy whodunit can have no death at all.
