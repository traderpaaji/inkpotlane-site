How to Choose a Word of the Year: From Twenty Words to One

How to choose a word of the year that still means something in March: write twenty candidates, cut them with four tests, then give the one left a place to live.

Inkpot Lane · 10 November 2026 · 7 min read

A word of the year is the smallest plan there is. No list, no spreadsheet, just one word, written somewhere you will see it, that you hold up against the year's decisions as they come: will this help me rest, or not? Choosing it is the hard part. The first word that comes to mind might be a good one, but it is worth testing against the year you have actually had and the decisions you expect to make. Here is how to choose a word of the year that is still working in spring, by starting with twenty and letting four simple tests do the cutting.

The short answer: Write down twenty candidate words without judging them. Narrow the list by asking what you could do because of each word on an ordinary Tuesday, what it answers about the year just gone, whether it sounds like you, and what picture or action it suggests. Keep the word that feels most useful, then put it where you will see it: on a vision board, in your planner, or on the first page of the year.

This is not the dictionary's word of the year

Dictionaries and language societies choose words of the year too. The American Dialect Society has voted on one since 1990, at its conference each January; Merriam-Webster's selections go back to 2003 and Collins's to 2013, and Oxford University Press announces its own choices too. Their word tries to capture the language of a year that has just happened, for everybody.

A personal word of the year works the other way round. It describes the year that is about to happen, for one person. It is not a prediction; it is a direction. "Brave" will not make 2027 brave. It gives you a question to ask when a brave choice turns up.

How to choose a word of the year in four steps

Step 1: Write twenty, quickly

Set a timer for ten minutes. Write down every word that comes to mind for how you would like next year to feel or to be. Do not stop to judge. Verbs, nouns and adjectives all count. Twenty is a useful target because it gives you room to go beyond your first few ideas. It is not a rule; if you have fewer, work with the words that matter to you.

If you are stuck, three prompts help:

  • What do I want next year to feel like? (calm, full, light, bold)
  • What did this year have too much of? (noise, rushing, worry) and what is its opposite? (quiet, slow, trust)
  • What would I do more of if nobody was watching? (make, write, play, rest)

Here is an example list of twenty, the kind ten minutes can produce: calm, brave, focus, rest, home, simplify, trust, grow, health, joy, slow, enough, create, finish, steady, open, rebuild, light, gather, begin.

Step 2: Cut with the Tuesday test

Read each word and ask: on an ordinary Tuesday in February, what would I do because of this word? If you cannot name one action, cross it out.

Simplify passes: say no to one thing this week. Finish passes: finish the half-done thing first. Light is lovely, but it is hard to say what it asks of you on a Tuesday. Health is a topic, not a direction; rest or steady turns it into one.

From our twenty, this test leaves about ten: focus, rest, simplify, trust, slow, enough, create, finish, gather, begin.

Step 3: Ask what it answers

A word that answers the year you just had has a reason to exist. Look back over 2026 and ask: what was missing, or what was too much? Then keep the words on your list that answer it.

If this year felt crowded, simplify, enough and slow might answer it. If you started more things than you finished, finish and focus might fit. A year of wanting more company could point to gather. For our worked example, imagine that 2026 was both crowded and full of unfinished projects: of the ten words left, four now stand out, simplify, focus, finish and enough. The others are not bad words; they just fit this particular year less well. If you want a structured way to look back, a wheel of life assessment scores eight areas in ten minutes and shows where the year was thin.

Step 4: Say it out loud, then picture it

Say each remaining word aloud in a sentence: "This is my year to finish." "This is my year to simplify." Notice which feels natural and which feels borrowed. You may end up with two good candidates rather than one obvious winner; keep both for the last test.

Then try turning each word into a picture or a concrete action. Simplify might be a clear shelf and fewer commitments; finish might be the last page of a notebook. In our example simplify wins, because it answers the crowded year, suggests everyday actions and brings a clear picture to mind. That takes twenty candidates to one. If your best word is harder to picture, keep it anyway; what matters is that you know how you will use it.

Give the word a place to live

Once you have chosen your word, give yourself a few ways to come back to it during the year. You could put it in three places:

  • Where you look every day. On the fridge, inside a wardrobe door, as a phone lock screen, or at the top of a vision board.
  • Where you plan. At the top of each week's page, so it is there when you decide what the week is for.
  • In a question. Turn it into one sentence you can ask about any choice: "Would this simplify my week, or add another commitment?"

Then check in on it, the same way you would a goal. At the end of March, June and September, ask whether the word is still doing its job. It is allowed to change. Choosing a better word in April is the method working, not failing.

Frequently asked questions

How do you choose a word of the year?

Write down candidate words quickly, then test them: what could you do because of each one on an ordinary day, what does it answer about the year just gone, and how natural does it feel to say? If several still fit, choose the one that gives you the clearest direction; a picture in your mind helps, but is not essential.

What are some good words of the year?

Words that suggest an action are the easiest to use day to day: simplify, finish, focus, rest, gather, begin, trust, slow, enough, create. The best word is the one that answers your own past year, not the most popular one.

Can I change my word of the year?

Yes. Check in at the end of each quarter. If the word has stopped helping you make choices, choosing a better one is part of the process.

Is a word of the year the same as a resolution?

No. A resolution is usually a specific target. A word of the year is a direction you use to judge choices as they come, and it works well alongside one or two specific goals.
