Difference Between a Hymn and a Carol: 6 Christmas Songs Tested

The difference between a hymn and a carol, explained simply, then tested on six Christmas favorites, from Hark! the Herald to The Holly and the Ivy.

Inkpot Lane · 10 November 2026 · 9 min read

A Christmas carol sheet might print "Hark! the Herald Angels Sing" a few lines above "The Holly and the Ivy," both under the heading "Carols." One was written by a Methodist hymn writer as a hymn for Christmas Day. The other is a traditional carol whose beginnings nobody can date with confidence. The difference between a hymn and a carol is real, but familiar Christmas songs do not always fit neatly into one category, and the way they came to be sung side by side is the interesting part.

The short answer: A hymn is a religious song written for worship or praise. A carol is a festive song, historically linked with singing and sometimes dancing, and today especially linked with Christmas. Their histories differ, but the labels overlap. Many of the best-known Christmas "carols," such as "Hark! the Herald Angels Sing," "Joy to the World" and "O Come, All Ye Faithful," began as hymns and became carols through use. A few, such as "The Holly and the Ivy," were folk carols from the start.

The difference between a hymn and a carol, in two definitions

A hymn takes its name from the Greek hymnos, a song of praise. It is a song written for worship, for adoration or prayer, and typically addressed to God. In the English-speaking churches the hymn as we know it grew in the eighteenth century: Isaac Watts helped make the freely written hymn, not only a sung version of a psalm, a central part of Protestant worship, and Charles Wesley's hymns became central to Methodist worship and spread far beyond it.

A carol comes from a different place. The word is traced to the Old French carole, a circle dance with singers, and in its medieval form a carol was a festive song, generally religious but not tied to the church's own services. The earliest English collection of Christmas carols, John Awdlay's manuscript of 1426, records twenty-five "caroles of Cristemas." In 1928 Percy Dearmer, one of the editors of The Oxford Book of Carols, put it in one line: "Carols are songs with a religious impulse that are simple, hilarious, popular, and modern." Hilarious, in his day, meant cheerful.

So the two words stress different things. "Hymn" stresses a song's devotional purpose; "carol" stresses its festive character and its history among the people who sang it. Neither label tells the whole story of where or how a song has been sung, which is why a single song can be both.

Why the line blurred

For a long stretch of English history, carols were sung mostly outside the church's own services, often by singers going from house to house. Parliament's restrictions on Christmas in 1647 disrupted public celebrations, but many old carols carried on as folk songs. In the nineteenth century Victorian collectors and new carol books brought them back into wider use, and in the late 1870s cathedrals began adding carols to their Christmas services; St Paul's Cathedral in London did so at Christmas 1878. On Christmas Eve 1880, Edward White Benson, Bishop of Truro in Cornwall, held the first formal service of Nine Lessons and Carols, a pattern King's College, Cambridge, adopted in 1918 and made familiar around the world. Once carols entered church use, as Wikipedia's history of the Christmas carol puts it, "the words 'hymn' and 'carol' were used almost interchangeably."

Hark! the Herald Angels Sing: a hymn that became the carol

Charles Wesley's text first appeared in Hymns and Sacred Poems (1739), compiled with his brother John, under the plain title "Hymn for Christmas-Day." It was a hymn in every sense. Two things turned it into the carol we know. In 1753 the preacher George Whitefield cut and revised it, including its opening couplet. And in the mid-1850s William Hayman Cummings, organist at Waltham Abbey Church in England, fitted the words to music from Felix Mendelssohn's Festgesang of 1840, a secular piece written for a festival celebrating the printing press. A hymn text and a festival tune: together they became the carol sung today.

Joy to the World: not written for Christmas at all

Isaac Watts published it in 1719 in The Psalms of David Imitated in the Language of the New Testament, under the title "The Messiah's Coming and Kingdom," as a Christian reading of Psalm 98. Wikipedia notes it is widely believed Watts did not write it to be sung only at Christmas. The tune most people know came more than a century later, from the American Lowell Mason, who published it under the name "Antioch" in the 1830s and arranged it again in 1848, attributing it "from Handel." It is a hymn by birth, and a Christmas carol by long habit.

O Come, All Ye Faithful: a hymn in two languages

The original is the Latin hymn Adeste Fideles. In modern hymnals it is usually credited to John Francis Wade, whose manuscript copies date from roughly the 1740s and 1750s; the earliest surviving copies with secure dates are from 1750, and a 1751 copy is held by Stonyhurst College. Whether Wade composed it or copied it is not settled. The English version grew from a translation Frederick Oakeley made around 1841, while he was still a Church of England clergyman (he became a Catholic in 1845); the familiar opening line, "O come, all ye faithful," first appeared in an 1852 hymnal. It is still printed in hymnals as a hymn, and still sung on carol sheets as a carol, often with the same verses.

Silent Night: written for a service, spread as a folk song

Joseph Mohr's 1816 poem was set to music by Franz Xaver Gruber on 24 December 1818 and sung that night at a church service in Oberndorf, with a guitar. Then traveling singers from the Tyrol carried it across Europe, and its first printing, in 1833, presented it as a Tyrolean folk song with no names attached. It sits right on the line, which is perhaps why it belongs to everyone. The full story of Silent Night is told separately.

Away in a Manger: a children's carol

It first appears in print in 1868, in an American church newspaper, with no author named, and it grew up in Sunday-school songbooks. It was never a hymn of the church's main services; it was a song for small children, and it is a carol in the plainest sense. Its long-repeated link to Martin Luther does not hold up, as we explain in who wrote "Away in a Manger".

The Holly and the Ivy: a carol from the start

Its words survive on nineteenth-century printed sheets, including broadsides from Birmingham; an older broadside was reported in the 1860s, but its date, and the carol's beginnings, remain uncertain. The tune sung today was collected in 1909 by the folk-song collector Cecil Sharp from a singer named Mary Clayton in Chipping Campden, Gloucestershire, and published in 1911. Before that it varied from village to village. Its documented history runs through printed sheets and singers, not back to a known hymn writer or a particular church: a carol in the oldest sense.

So which is it when you sing?

Neither label is wrong. If a song was written to be sung by a congregation in worship, it is a hymn, whatever is printed above it. If it lives in the season, at the door, around a piano, in a school hall, it is being used as a carol, whatever it was written for. Several of the Christmas songs sung most often began as hymns and became carols as well, without ever stopping being hymns.

That also explains why carol books and hymnals print the same songs with slightly different words. Each editor chooses stanzas, translations and revisions; to understand a particular wording, the useful question is which printing it follows.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a hymn and a carol?

A hymn is a religious song written for worship or praise. A carol is a festive song, historically linked with singing and sometimes dancing, and today especially linked with Christmas. The labels overlap: many Christmas carols began as hymns and are still sung as both.

Is "Silent Night" a hymn or a carol?

Both. It was written for a Christmas Eve church service in 1818, but it spread as a Tyrolean folk song and is sung today mainly as a carol.

Is "Joy to the World" a Christmas hymn?

It was written by Isaac Watts in 1719 as a hymn based on Psalm 98, and it is widely believed he did not mean it only for Christmas. It became a Christmas carol through use.

Why do carol books and hymnals print different words for the same song?

Each keeps the version its own singers use. Hymnals often update wording; older carol books keep earlier versions. Both may be faithful to a real printing of the song.
