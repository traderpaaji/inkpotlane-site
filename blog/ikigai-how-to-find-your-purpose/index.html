The Ikigai Diagram Isn't Japanese: What Ikigai Really Means

The ikigai diagram wasn't drawn in Japan; a blogger built it in 2014. What ikigai actually means, and four honest lists to find your own.

Inkpot Lane · 10 September 2026 · 6 min read

You have probably seen the ikigai diagram: four overlapping circles labeled what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs and what you can be paid for, with the word ikigai sitting in the middle where they all meet. It hangs on office walls, fills slide decks and turns up in countless posts about finding your purpose. It is a useful drawing. It just isn't Japanese.

The short answer: Ikigai is a Japanese word for what makes life feel worth living. In Japan it is often something small and everyday, like a morning walk or a grandchild. The four-circle diagram came much later: a blogger drew it in 2014 by putting the word on a Western chart about purpose.

The myth: ikigai is the center of four circles

The popular version goes like this. Find the one thing that you love, that you are good at, that the world needs and that someone will pay you for, and you have found your ikigai, your reason for being. Anything less is a near miss: passion without pay, or a profession without love.

It is an appealing idea, and to be fair to the diagram, it does make people think. But it quietly turns ikigai into a career test, and it sets a bar most people will never clear. If your reason for getting up in the morning is not also your salary, the diagram says you are not there yet.

Where the ikigai diagram really came from

The story starts with a different chart. In 2011, the Spanish author Andrés Zuzunaga drew a Venn diagram about purpose, with four overlapping circles, according to Ikigai Tribe. There was no Japanese word in it at all.

A few years later, a blogger named Marc Winn came across ikigai through Dan Buettner's work on the Blue Zones, the places where people tend to live unusually long lives. There, the word was offered as "a reason for being." In 2014, one Wednesday morning, Winn wrote a blog post that put Zuzunaga's diagram and the Japanese word together, with ikigai in the middle where "purpose" had been (Winn).

The post spread. The diagram was copied, recolored and reposted, and within a few years it was, for many people outside Japan, simply what ikigai meant.

To his credit, Winn has since said so himself. He now calls the four circles "a Western purpose diagram wearing a Japanese name."

What ikigai means in Japan

The word joins iki, to live, and kai, worth or effect. Put together, it means something like "what makes life worth living."

In everyday Japanese it tends to be modest. Winn's own examples of what it more often means are a morning walk, a grandchild, a garden. Japanese researchers have written about it for decades. The psychiatrist Mieko Kamiya's 1966 book Ikigai ni Tsuite (On the Meaning of Life), written after years working with patients at a sanatorium, is a foundational work, and the Osaka University researcher Noriyuki Nakanishi has noted that ikigai is not necessarily tied to economic status. Japanese surveys cited by Ikigai Tribe find people naming several sources at once, with good food, travel and music near the top.

In other words, ikigai is less a single target at the center of four circles and more a collection: the small things that make an ordinary Tuesday feel worth it. You may already have more of it than the diagram gives you credit for.

A better way to find your ikigai

None of this makes the four circles useless. They are a genuinely good tool for thinking about work. The mistake is treating them as the definition. So start with the Japanese meaning, and use the circles as a second step.

First, list the small things

Before anything else, write down ten small things that make a day feel worth living. The first coffee on the step. The walk to the station. A voice you love on the phone. Cooking for friends. Don't rank them. Just notice how many you already have.

Then, make four honest lists

Give yourself ten minutes for each. Write fast, and don't judge.

  1. Things I love doing. Not what you think you should love. What makes you lose track of time? What did you love at ten years old?
  2. Things I am good at. Include the small, unglamorous skills: explaining things simply, calming people down, cooking for twenty. Ask a friend if you are stuck; other people often see our strengths more clearly than we do.
  3. What the people around me need. Start close to home: your family, your street, your workplace. What do people keep asking you for?
  4. What someone would pay me for. Be practical. What have you been paid for before? What could you learn that someone would value?

Look for what repeats

Read the lists side by side and circle anything that appears on more than one, including your list of small things. Maybe "explaining things simply" is on your good-at list, your niece needs help with math, and you love the moment someone finally understands. That is an overlap worth noticing, and it still counts if it never earns a cent.

Write your best guess at your ikigai as one sentence. It does not have to be perfect. It has to be true enough to act on.

Don't force it to become a goal

This is where the popular version of ikigai often sneaks back in. You find something that makes life meaningful, and immediately ask how to measure it, monetize it or turn it into an achievement.

You do not have to.

If Sunday breakfast with your father makes life feel worth living, the correct next step may simply be to protect Sunday breakfast. If it is gardening, music or walking the dog before the street wakes up, it can remain exactly that.

Some discoveries will point toward a goal. Others are things a goal should make room for.

That is how we use the ikigai page in The Life I'm Building: as a page for noticing what matters before the goal-setting pages begin. The life wheel and 9-grid come later. They are different tools for different questions.

Ikigai asks, "What makes an ordinary life worth being in?"

A goal asks, "What do I want to change or build?"

They do not have to be the same answer.

Frequently asked questions

What does ikigai mean?

Ikigai is a Japanese word meaning roughly "what makes life worth living." In everyday Japanese it often refers to small, ordinary things, such as family, a hobby or a daily walk, rather than one grand life purpose.

Is the ikigai Venn diagram Japanese?

No. The four-circle diagram comes from a Venn diagram about purpose by Andrés Zuzunaga, which the blogger Marc Winn relabeled with the word ikigai in 2014.

Does ikigai have to be my job?

No. Many people's ikigai has nothing to do with how they earn a living. The four circles can help you think about work, but they are one tool, not the definition.

How do I find my ikigai?

Start by listing the small things that make a day worth living. Then make four lists (what you love, what you are good at, what the people around you need and what someone would pay you for) and circle what repeats.
