Christmas Short Stories for Adults: 5 Classics by Reading Time

Christmas short stories for adults, from a 10-minute O. Henry to an evening with Dickens, each measured by word count, and where to read them free.

Inkpot Lane · 10 November 2026 · 7 min read

There is a particular kind of December evening that wants a story but not a novel. The dishes are done, the lights are on, and there are perhaps twenty minutes before someone needs the sofa. A novel asks for a week; a story can be finished tonight. These Christmas short stories for adults, and two longer works worth including, are arranged by estimated reading time, so you can choose one that fits your evening. We counted the words in each text and worked out the times at a pace of 200 words a minute: the word counts are measurements, the times are estimates.

The short answer: Five classic Christmas reads, shortest first, at an estimated 200 words a minute: three short stories, one chapter from a novel and one novella. O. Henry's "The Gift of the Magi" (about 2,100 words, 10 minutes), Hans Christian Andersen's "The Fir-Tree" (about 3,200 words, 16 minutes), "A Merry Christmas," the second chapter of Little Women (about 4,000 words, 20 minutes), Arthur Conan Doyle's "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle" (about 7,800 words, 40 minutes) and Charles Dickens's A Christmas Carol (about 28,000 words, a long evening). All five are out of copyright and free to read on Project Gutenberg.

How we estimated the reading times

Each word count comes from the Project Gutenberg edition linked below, counted from the story's title to its last line, without the catalog and license text around it, then rounded; dividing by 200 gives the estimated time. Reading speed varies a great deal from person to person, so the minutes are only a guide: 200 words a minute is a comfortable pace for reading fiction slowly, and reading aloud is slower again. Use the word counts to compare the stories with each other rather than to time yourself.

Christmas short stories for adults, shortest first

1. "The Gift of the Magi," O. Henry (about 10 minutes)

First published in the New York Sunday World on 10 December 1905, and collected the next spring in The Four Million. A young couple in a cheap New York flat have almost no money and one day left to buy each other a present. It opens with Della counting her savings, $1.87, three times over, and the ending is famous enough that you may know it already. Read it anyway: O. Henry makes one small Christmas exchange carry a whole love story.

Best for: a short reading break, or reading aloud to someone you love.

2. "The Fir-Tree," Hans Christian Andersen (about 16 minutes)

Published in Copenhagen on 21 December 1844 among Andersen's New Fairy Tales. A young fir tree in the forest is so eager to grow up, to be chosen, to reach the next thing, that it never enjoys the moment it is in, and the story follows it through one Christmas and beyond. It is published among Andersen's fairy tales for children, but its themes of impatience and regret give adult readers plenty to think about too.

Best for: a quiet evening alone, and anyone who spends December rushing toward Christmas.

3. "A Merry Christmas," Louisa May Alcott (about 20 minutes)

The second chapter of Little Women, whose first volume was published in 1868. On Christmas morning the four March sisters, with their father away at the war, are asked to give their own breakfast to a poor family nearby. It stands on its own as a short story, and it has the warmth of a novel without asking you to read the novel.

Best for: sharing with a teenager, or with the person who first read you Little Women.

4. "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle," Arthur Conan Doyle (about 40 minutes)

First published in The Strand Magazine in January 1892. Two days after Christmas, Dr. Watson calls on Sherlock Holmes to wish him the compliments of the season and finds him studying a battered old hat. A stolen jewel turns up in a most unlikely place, and Holmes follows its trail through a London Christmas of geese, markets and pubs. It is the classic Christmas mystery in the short form, and the one to read if you like to solve along. We say more about it, and about longer Christmas mysteries, in our guide to Christmas mystery books for adults.

Best for: an evening by the fire, and anyone who likes to guess before the detective explains.

5. A Christmas Carol, Charles Dickens (a long evening)

Published in London by Chapman & Hall on 19 December 1843, and sold out by Christmas Eve. Strictly it is a novella, about 28,000 words in five short chapters that Dickens called staves, so it is not a one-sitting read for most people. But each stave works as an evening on its own: read one a night from the 20th and the last lands on Christmas Eve. If you know the story mainly from films, the original is worth discovering: Dickens's dialogue, humor and ghost scenes have their own character on the page.

Best for: five evenings in a row, or a whole snowed-in night.

How to make it an evening

  • Read aloud. These stories can be a pleasure aloud, especially if you share out the dialogue. Reading aloud takes longer than reading to yourself, so pick a shorter story than you think.
  • One a night. Read each of the first four on its own evening, then give A Christmas Carol five evenings of its own, one stave at a time. Or simply choose one for tonight.
  • Stop before the end. In "The Blue Carbuncle," pause when a small, nervous man starts pestering the goose seller at Covent Garden Market, and ask everyone why he might care so much about a goose. Then read on and see what Holmes makes of it.

Where to read them

All five are in the public domain in the United States, because they were published long before 1931, and Project Gutenberg offers each one free to read online or on an e-reader: The Gift of the Magi, Andersen's Fairy Tales ("The Fir Tree"), Little Women, The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes ("The Blue Carbuncle") and A Christmas Carol. Copyright rules differ from country to country, so readers outside the US should check their local rules.

Frequently asked questions

What is the shortest classic Christmas story for adults?

Of the classics here, O. Henry's "The Gift of the Magi" is the shortest, about 2,100 words, roughly ten minutes at a relaxed reading pace.

What is a good Christmas short story to read aloud?

"The Gift of the Magi" and "A Merry Christmas" from Little Women both read well aloud and finish in one sitting. A Christmas Carol works aloud one stave per evening.

Is there a Christmas Sherlock Holmes story?

Yes. "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle," first published in January 1892, is set just after Christmas and follows a stolen jewel and a Christmas goose. It takes about forty minutes to read.

Can I read these Christmas stories for free?

Yes, in the United States: all five were published long before 1931 and are in the public domain there, and Project Gutenberg offers them free to read online.
