How to Set Goals for the New Year: Four Dates That Carry 2027

How to set goals for the new year so they last past January: a December evening, a Monday start, an end-of-month check and a spring review.

Inkpot Lane · 10 November 2026 · 7 min read

You can write a new year's goal on 31 December and feel certain about it. But what happens on the ordinary days afterward? Without a first step, and a date to look at how it is going, even a carefully chosen goal is easy to lose sight of. If you want to know how to set goals for the new year that are still alive in spring, the answer is less about the goals themselves and more about the calendar: four dates, each with one small job. Put them in your diary now, and the year has a shape before it starts.

The short answer: Set new year goals across four dates, not one night. A December evening: look back at 2026, then choose one main goal and write it as something you will do, not something you will stop. Monday 4 January 2027: start with the smallest daily step. Sunday 31 January: a fifteen-minute check, where you adjust instead of quitting. Wednesday 31 March: a quarter review, where you decide what to push next. A large 2020 study of new year's resolutions found that people who framed their goals as something to approach did better than those who framed them as something to avoid.

Why one night is not enough

A goal chosen on 31 December can work well. The trouble comes if it is chosen without looking back at the year you actually had, or written down with a start date and nothing after it. When a week goes badly, a planned review gives you a way to adjust rather than abandon the goal.

The four dates below give you a way to deal with both. The first is for choosing the goal, the second for beginning it, and the last two for looking at what happened and adjusting what comes next, so that a bad week becomes something to adjust rather than a reason to stop.

How to set goals for the new year: date 1, a December evening

Pick an evening in the second half of December, with an hour free and a pen. Do not start with the new year. Start with the old one.

Look back first. Write down three things that went well in 2026, and three that did not. Then ask which area of life felt thinnest: work, health, money, friends, family, home, learning, rest. If you are not sure, a wheel of life assessment takes ten minutes and shows the lopsided spot on one page.

Choose a manageable number of goals. For this method, start with one main goal and a few smaller goals that support it. If you have other, unrelated priorities, write them down separately; the point is to be clear about what gets your attention first.

Write it as something to do, not something to stop. In 2020, the Swedish researchers Martin Oscarsson, Per Carlbring, Gerhard Andersson and Alexander Rozental published a study that followed 1,066 people who made new year's resolutions. In the one-year comparison of goal types, which covered 730 of them, 58.9% of people with approach-oriented goals (moving toward something) reported success, against 47.1% of those with avoidance-oriented goals (stopping or avoiding something). Overall, about 55% of those who answered at one year said they had kept their resolution. These were people's own assessments, and nobody was assigned a goal type at random, so the comparison does not prove that rewording a goal causes success. It is still a practical reason to try saying what you will do.

Here is what the rewrite looks like:

Avoidance goalSame goal, written to approach
Stop ordering takeawayCook dinner at home four nights a week
Stop scrolling in bedRead ten pages before lights out
Spend lessPut a set amount into savings on the 1st of every month
Stop being so busyKeep Sunday afternoon free, every week

The approach version tells you what to do on an ordinary Tuesday. The avoidance version only tells you when you have failed.

Make it specific enough to check. "Cook more" cannot be checked. "Cook dinner at home four nights a week" can be ticked off every Sunday.

Date 2: Monday 4 January 2027, the smallest start

New Year's Day 2027 falls on a Friday, and the first full Monday-to-Sunday week begins on 4 January. If your weeks start on a Monday, that is a convenient day to begin; if the 1st suits you better, start then. What matters is a definite first action with a date on it.

Break the main goal down until there is one step small enough to do in ten minutes today. For the cooking goal: on Monday, choose four dinners for the week and write the shopping list. That is all. If you want the full method for turning one big goal into steps you can actually start, how to break down a goal into steps walks through it.

Date 3: Sunday 31 January, the fifteen-minute check

By the end of January you will have had several weeks to try your plan, and a short review shows which parts you have kept up and where the plan needs changing. Put fifteen minutes in the diary for the last Sunday of January and answer four questions in writing:

  1. What did I actually do toward the goal this month?
  2. Which week went best, and why?
  3. What got in the way?
  4. What will I change for February?

Your answer to question four might be a small adjustment: fewer nights, a different day, a simpler first step. If the goal still matters, keep it and change the plan. If you missed a week or two, start again on the next Monday rather than waiting for a fresh month. Our 15-minute Sunday routine shows how to restart after a missed week without starting over.

Date 4: Wednesday 31 March, the quarter review

Three months is long enough to see a pattern and short enough to change course. At the end of March, look at the whole quarter: which supporting goals moved, which stalled, and which no longer matter. Then decide what to push for the next three months. Choose a few things to push hard, keep the rest ticking over with a small daily step, and pause anything that must honestly wait. A paused goal is a decision, not a failure.

The Oscarsson study also tested different amounts of support. Participants were randomly placed in three groups, and the group given a moderate package (naming a person to support them, monthly follow-ups and an email about coping with setbacks) reported better results than either of the other two, including the group given the most. The study did not test the four-date plan in this article, and it could not say which part of that package made the difference. These four dates are a practical structure to try, not a research-tested schedule.

Frequently asked questions

How do you set goals for the new year?

Look back at the past year first, choose one main goal, and write it as something you will do rather than something you will stop. Then mark three more dates: the first Monday of January to begin, the end of January to check and adjust, and the end of March to review the quarter.

What is an approach goal?

A goal written as something to do or move toward, such as "cook at home four nights a week," rather than something to avoid, such as "stop ordering takeaway." In a 2020 study of new year's resolutions, people with approach goals were more likely to report success after a year.

How many new year goals should I set?

Fewer than you think. One main goal, with a few smaller goals that support it, is easier to keep track of than a long list, and it makes the monthly check quick.

What should I do if I miss a week?

Start again the next Monday. A missed week is information about the plan, not a verdict on the goal. Use your end-of-month check to make the plan easier to keep.
