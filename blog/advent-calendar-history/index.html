Advent Calendar History: From Chalk Lines to a 25-Day Puzzle

Advent calendar history in short: Lutheran chalk lines, a Munich print shop in 1908, post-war doors, and how to run your own 25-day countdown with puzzles.

Inkpot Lane · 12 November 2026 · 6 min read

In nineteenth-century Germany, some Lutheran families counted the days to Christmas with nothing more than a piece of chalk. Each morning in December a child drew one more line on a door or a wall, and the lines marched toward the twenty-fourth. Other families lit one candle a day instead. That is the plain beginning of advent calendar history: a household that wanted the waiting to be visible. Everything in the shop window today, the cardboard doors, the chocolate, the tiny toys, grew from those lines.

The short answer: The advent calendar began as a family way of counting down to Christmas in nineteenth-century German Lutheran homes, with chalk marks or a candle a day. One history society dates the earliest known handmade wooden calendar to 1851. A printer named Gerhard Lang, working at the Reichhold and Lang printing office in Munich, is credited with mass-producing calendars close to today's form in 1908. After the Second World War, printers in Germany began making them again under occupation licenses, and fold-out doors replaced tear-off sheets by about 1950.

The earliest advent calendar was a line on a wall

"Advent" means the weeks before Christmas, and the Church counts them as four Sundays. A calendar that runs from 1 December is something slightly different: a count of days, not Sundays. The Institute of Heraldic and Genealogical Studies, in a short history of the custom, says German Lutherans in the 1800s began counting down by burning a candle a day or by marking a wall with chalk. The same page dates the first known handmade wooden advent calendar to 1851.

Be careful with the word "invented." No single person is recorded as the inventor of the first calendar, and the early ones were homemade, which is why so few survive to be dated. What the record supports is a custom that grew up in homes first and reached shops later.

1908: a print shop in Munich

The step from a family habit to a thing you could buy is usually credited to Gerhard Lang, who worked at the Reichhold and Lang printing office in Munich. The Institute's page says he mass-produced calendars in a form close to today's in 1908. You will find other, more colorful versions of the Lang story online, including a childhood calendar made by his mother, but those details turn up mostly on pages that cite nobody, so they are left out here.

The little doors that open came later. Wikipedia's account of the post-war years says tear-off calendars were popular at first and that fold-out doors had largely replaced them by about 1950.

After the war: the doors open

The calendar came back to German shops almost as soon as printing did. Wikipedia's account says that by 1945 calendars were being produced again in all the occupation zones, mostly from designs dating to around 1930, and that in December 1945 a Stuttgart printer named Richard Sellmer received a license from the American occupation authorities to print 50,000 of them. Between 1946 and 1998 his firm produced more than 230 different calendars.

Radio and television took up the idea too. In Sweden, a children's radio program built as an advent calendar began in 1957, with a televised version in 1960; Denmark's first followed in 1962.

How many doors, and when do you start?

Most calendars have 24 doors, one for each day up to Christmas Eve, though some add the Sundays or run on to 6 January. The start date depends on the maker. Reusable paper and wooden calendars usually begin on 1 December. Others follow the Church year and begin on the first Sunday of Advent, which falls between 27 November and 3 December. In 2026 that Sunday is 29 November, because Christmas Day falls on a Friday.

Wikipedia gives no date for the first sweet-filled calendar, and neither do the other sources used here, so be wary of any page that offers a confident year for it.

Make your own countdown: a puzzle a day

The old calendars had a simple rule: one small thing a day, and no skipping ahead. That rule works for puzzles as well as for pictures. Here is a plan that takes a household about ten minutes a day.

  1. Choose a container. An envelope per day, a numbered row of clothespins, or just a pencil tick on a printed page. The calendar is only a way of making tomorrow worth waiting for.
  2. Make the puzzles easier at first and harder as December goes on. An early puzzle should be solved in a minute. By the twentieth, a solver should have earned something tougher.
  3. Put a small reward at the end of each one, such as a line to read aloud or a fact to tell the table. The solving is the treat; the line is the proof.
  4. Skip the days you miss. A countdown is a mood, not a debt. Pick up at today's date.

To try it, here is day one in the form of a cryptogram, a puzzle in which each letter has been swapped for another. It is a line from "A Visit from St. Nicholas," printed in 1823. Three letters are given: K stands for A, V for O and X for L.

JBDDG WQDULAJKL AV KXX, KZP AV KXX K FVVP ZUFQA.

Frequently asked questions

Who invented the advent calendar?

No one person is recorded as its inventor. German Lutheran families counted down with chalk marks and candles in the 1800s; a history society dates the earliest known handmade wooden calendar to 1851; and Gerhard Lang of the Reichhold and Lang printing office in Munich is credited with mass-producing printed calendars in 1908.

How many days does an advent calendar have?

Most have 24 doors, one for each day up to Christmas Eve. Some add the Sundays of Advent, and some run on to 6 January. A calendar for 1 December to Christmas Day has 25 days.

When should you start an advent calendar?

Reusable calendars usually begin on 1 December. Calendars that follow the Church year begin on the first Sunday of Advent, which is between 27 November and 3 December; in 2026 it is 29 November.

Is an advent calendar a religious object?

It began in Lutheran homes as a way of marking the weeks before Christmas, and many calendars still carry religious pictures. Today there are also calendars built around food, toys, books and puzzles, so the choice is up to the household.
