Wheel of Life Assessment: The Wheel That Wouldn't Roll

A wheel of life assessment takes ten minutes: score eight areas of your life, join the dots, see where the shape bumps, then turn the biggest gap into one goal.

Inkpot Lane · 10 September 2026 · 6 min read

Draw a circle and cut it into eight slices, like a pie. Label them Health, Money, Work or study, Relationships, Growth, Fun, Home and Purpose. Score each slice from 1 to 10 for how it feels today, join the dots, and look at the shape. If it would bump along rather than roll, good: that is exactly what a wheel of life assessment is for.

The short answer: A wheel of life assessment is a ten-minute exercise. You score eight areas of your life from 1 to 10, mark the scores on a circle and join the dots. The shape shows where life is lopsided, and the biggest gap between "now" and "where I want it" is usually the goal worth a year.

Before: busy every day, and somehow off course

Picture a Thursday evening in November. You have been busy every day for months. The calendar is full, the inbox is never quite empty, and you would struggle to name a week that felt wasted. And yet something is off.

The gym bag has been in the car since September. There is a friend you keep meaning to call, and the silence has lasted long enough that calling now feels awkward. The guitar is in the corner, slightly out of tune. None of this is a crisis. It is just that the things you once said mattered most have slipped to the bottom of the list, and the list is run by whatever shouts loudest.

The trouble with this feeling is that it is vague. "Everything is a bit off" does not tell you what to change, so you change nothing. Or you try to change everything at once on the first of January, and give up by February.

After: one honest shape and one choice

Now picture the same evening, ten minutes later. On the table is a lopsided shape. Work is out near the rim. Health and Fun are pushed in toward the center, like a dent in a tire. For the first time in months, "a bit off" has become something specific: it's these two.

That is the whole after. Your life has not changed yet. But you have a picture of where it is, a second shape showing where you would honestly like it to be by the end of the year, and one gap you have chosen to work on. The rest can wait, and you know why it can wait.

A few months later you draw it again, and one dent has filled in a little. That is the moment that makes small daily actions feel worth the trouble.

The bridge: how to do a wheel of life assessment

The exercise is commonly credited to Paul J. Meyer, who founded the Success Motivation Institute and is said to have created it in the 1960s (BetterUp). Coaches have used versions of it ever since. You do not need a coach. You need a pen, ten minutes and some honesty.

The eight areas

  • Health: sleep, energy, movement, how your body feels most days.
  • Money: not how much you have, but how settled you feel about it.
  • Work or study: whether your working hours feel well spent.
  • Relationships: partner, family, friends, the people you would call at 2 a.m.
  • Growth: learning, reading, getting better at something you care about.
  • Fun: play, hobbies, the things you do for no reason at all.
  • Home: the space you live in and how it feels to walk through the door.
  • Purpose: whether your days add up to something that feels like yours.

Different versions use different slices. Swap any that do not fit your life.

Six steps

  1. Score each area from 1 to 10 for today. Go with your first instinct; overthinking turns everything into a 6.
  2. Mark each score on its slice, with 1 at the center and 10 at the rim.
  3. Join the dots and look at the shape before you judge it.
  4. In a second color, mark where you honestly want each area by year end. Not 10 everywhere.
  5. Measure the gaps between the two shapes, area by area.
  6. Circle the biggest gap and write one change that would move it.

Reading the shape

  • A small, even circle often means a tired year. Nothing is broken, but nothing has much energy. Rest may be the first goal.
  • A big, lopsided shape usually means one area is eating the others, very often work.
  • One deep dent is useful. It is usually where a small change makes the biggest difference to how the whole year feels.

If a score is hard to pin down, ask a blunt question. Health: how many nights last week did I wake up rested? Relationships: who have I been meaning to call for months? Fun: when did I last lose track of time? Purpose: if this year repeated ten times, would I be glad? If Purpose turns out to be the dent, an ikigai exercise is a gentle place to start.

A worked example (hypothetical)

Nadia is invented, not a customer; she is here to show how the numbers turn into a decision. Here is her wheel for today, with her year-end target in parentheses:

  • Health 4 (7)
  • Money 6 (7)
  • Work or study 8 (8)
  • Relationships 5 (7)
  • Growth 6 (7)
  • Fun 3 (6)
  • Home 6 (6)
  • Purpose 5 (7)

Two gaps tie at three points: Health and Fun. Nadia picks Health, because she suspects that more sleep and energy would lift Fun as well, and because Health gives her a goal she could prove: walk or run 5 km without stopping by September 30.

Now she has one change for this week (a twenty-minute walk after lunch, three days) and one goal for the year. The goal goes in the center of a 9 grid goal chart, where it is broken into eight supports and 64 actions of five to twenty minutes each. Fun gets a small, separate promise: one evening a week for the thing she does for no reason at all.

Notice what she did not do. She did not try to lift all eight areas. The wheel's job is not to make you score 10 everywhere. Its job is to help you choose.

Keeping the wheel where you will see it again

A wheel drawn on the back of an envelope works. The risk is the envelope. Most wheels get drawn once and lost, and it is the second drawing, the one that shows a dent filling in, where the exercise pays off.

Frequently asked questions

What is a wheel of life assessment?

A ten-minute exercise in which you score each area of your life from 1 to 10, mark the scores on a circle and join the dots. The shape shows which areas are strong, which are neglected and where a goal would help most.

Who created the wheel of life?

It is commonly credited to Paul J. Meyer, founder of the Success Motivation Institute, in the 1960s. Many coaches have adapted it since, so you will find versions with different areas.

What are the categories on a wheel of life?

There is no fixed list. A common set of eight is health, money, work or study, relationships, growth, fun, home and purpose. Change any that do not fit your life.

How often should I do a wheel of life?

At the start of the year and again every quarter works well. Keep the old wheels so you can see the shape change.
