The Blue Carbuncle: A Christmas Mystery You Can Almost Solve

The Blue Carbuncle is Sherlock Holmes's Christmas story: a lost hat, a goose and a stolen jewel. We checked how fair its clues are for the reader.

Inkpot Lane · 12 November 2026 · 7 min read

Sherlock Holmes's Christmas story begins with no crime at all. On the second morning after Christmas, Dr. Watson calls to wish his friend the compliments of the season and finds him on the sofa in a purple dressing-gown, beside a battered hat he has been examining with a lens. It belongs to nobody. A commissionaire named Peterson found it, together with a plump white goose, in the early hours of Christmas morning, when a man carrying the goose was set on by a gang of roughs in the street. "The Blue Carbuncle" is the Holmes story set at Christmas, and it has an unusual shape: a mystery that is really a chain, from a stolen jewel to a bird's crop, passed from hand to hand. The question is how fairly Conan Doyle lets you follow it.

The short answer: "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle" first appeared in The Strand Magazine in January 1892 and was collected in The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes that October, as the seventh of twelve stories. Its puzzle is not "who did it" so much as "how did the stone get into a goose." Doyle plays fair with the chain of sellers and gives the attentive reader a few real clues, chiefly a repeated date. He holds back the link that names the thief. This article spoils the whole story, so read it first if you can.

A spoiler warning. From here on, the culprit and the solution are given away. The story is in the public domain and short enough to read in an evening.

The chain, link by link

Here is the sequence of events, in the order they happened, not the order Doyle tells them.

On 22 December, a jewel known as the blue carbuncle is stolen from the jewel-case of the Countess of Morcar at the Hotel Cosmopolitan. A plumber named John Horner, who had been sent up to mend the grate in her dressing-room, is arrested. The stone is not found on him. The thief, in fact, is the hotel's head attendant, James Ryder, who has taken it away and, in a panic, pushed it down the throat of a goose belonging to his sister, who fattens fowls for market in Brixton Road. He means to take the bird away as a Christmas present and retrieve the stone later. But his sister has two white geese with barred tails and cannot tell them apart. He takes the wrong one, and the goose with the stone goes to market with the rest.

That goose goes to a Covent Garden dealer named Breckinridge, to the landlord of the Alpha Inn, to a goose club that gives each member a bird, and finally into the arms of a man named Henry Baker, who is carrying it home when the roughs attack him at about four o'clock on Christmas morning. He drops it, and Peterson picks it up. On the 27th, Peterson's wife finds the stone in the goose.

What a reader can really work out

Doyle makes it easy to follow the goose, because Holmes goes through the chain aloud. Four things are on the page before Holmes names the thief; the last is part of the chain rather than a clue to guilt.

  1. The date. When Holmes explains the robbery, he says it happened on 22 December, "just five days ago." Later, in Breckinridge's ledger, the last entry for Mrs. Oakshott of Brixton Road reads "December 22nd. Twenty-four geese." The geese that Breckinridge sold on to the Alpha Inn were bought on the very day of the theft. A sharp reader may notice the coincidence before Holmes says a word.
  2. The newspaper report. Holmes reads aloud the account from the police court. It says Ryder had shown Horner up, had stayed with him "some little time," and had then been called away; and that on his return the bureau had been forced and the casket lay empty. The man who left the plumber alone in the room is also the man who raised the alarm.
  3. The angry dealer. At Covent Garden, Breckinridge complains that people keep pestering him about his geese. Holmes remarks that other people besides themselves are anxious about the matter. Someone else is chasing the same birds. Moments later, a rat-faced man is shouting at the stall.
  4. The ledger as a map. The book shows the birds came from a town supplier, not from the country, so the trail must lead to Brixton Road.

What Doyle holds back

Three links are not on the page. Nothing says that Ryder has a sister, that the sister is Mrs. Oakshott, or that two geese looked alike. That the man hunting the geese is Ryder comes out only when Holmes names him in the market, and the explanation arrives in his own confession. So a careful reader can say with confidence that "someone connected with the hotel is hunting the goose," but cannot name him on the evidence.

That is a design choice rather than a fault. The story is not trying to be the kind of puzzle that rewards a reader for naming a culprit. Its pleasure lies in watching Holmes turn a small oddity, a lost hat and a bird, into a line that reaches a countess's bedroom.

The hat is the showpiece, not the puzzle

The famous part of the opening is Holmes's reading of the hat, from the size of the owner's head to the tallow stains that mean no gas in his house. It is entertaining, and each deduction comes with a visible reason. Holmes himself says some of his inferences are "distinct" and others only "a strong balance of probability," which is honest. Notice, though, that the story never returns to the most dramatic one. When Baker walks in, he fits much of the description, but nothing is said about his wife. Treat the hat as a character sketch, not a clue.

The kindest ending in the canon

Ryder confesses, weeping, and Holmes tells him to get out. He explains to Watson that Horner is the only person who might have been harmed, that the case against him will now collapse, and that sending Ryder to prison might make him a jailbird for life. "It is the season of forgiveness," Holmes says. He lets the thief go, and that act of mercy, more than the jewel, is what makes the story a Christmas one.

Frequently asked questions

When is The Blue Carbuncle set?

On the second morning after Christmas, which is 27 December, with the theft five days earlier on 22 December and the goose found at about four o'clock on Christmas morning.

Who stole the blue carbuncle?

James Ryder, the upper attendant at the Hotel Cosmopolitan, who took the stone from the Countess of Morcar's jewel-case. He let the plumber John Horner take the blame.

Why does Holmes let the thief go?

He says that Ryder will not appear against the plumber, so the case against Horner must fall, and that a prison sentence might turn a frightened man into a lifelong criminal. He adds that it is the season of forgiveness.

Is The Blue Carbuncle a good first Sherlock Holmes story?

It is short, self-contained and set at Christmas, and it needs no knowledge of the other stories, so many readers start there.
