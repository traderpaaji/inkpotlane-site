Copywork for Kids: How to Choose a Good Passage, by Age

Copywork for kids: compare six classic passages for length, reading level, punctuation and interest. Four simple checks help you choose a line to try.

Inkpot Lane · 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

Put two passages side by side. One is a short verse about rain falling on umbrellas and ships at sea. The other is a long sentence with three commas and several unfamiliar words. Which would you give a child who is still learning to write carefully? The shorter passage may be easier to manage, but length is not the only consideration. Good copywork for kids starts with choosing a passage that suits the child: words they can read, something interesting to notice, and enough room to write with care.

The short answer: A good copywork passage for kids is short enough to finish well in one sitting, made of words the child can already read, has one thing worth noticing (a rhyme, a comma, a vivid word), and is something the child actually likes. Below, six classic public-domain passages show how those four checks work with poems, sayings and prose of different lengths.

The four checks

Before comparing passages, here are the four questions we used. They are simple on purpose: you can ask them of any line in any book in under a minute.

  1. Length. Can the child copy it carefully, in their best handwriting, in about ten minutes? For a child starting copywork, try one or two lines; a more practiced writer may be ready for a short paragraph.
  2. Words. Can the child read and understand most of the words? If several are unfamiliar, read the passage together first or choose something simpler.
  3. Something to notice. Is there one feature worth pointing out: a rhyme, a capital letter for a name, quotation marks, an exclamation, a surprising word?
  4. Liking. Would the child choose it? Charlotte Mason, in Home Education, suggested letting children pick their own favorite verses, so the child's interest is worth considering alongside length and reading difficulty. We look at what Charlotte Mason wrote about copywork in a separate article.

Copywork for kids: six passages, compared

Every passage below is from a work published in the nineteenth or early twentieth century and in the public domain; we checked each one word for word against its Project Gutenberg text.

1. Robert Louis Stevenson, "Rain" (1885)

The rain is falling all around,
It falls on field and tree,
It rains on the umbrellas here,
And on the ships at sea.

From A Child's Garden of Verses, first published in 1885. Length: four short lines; the first two make a good first lesson, the whole verse a second. Words: all everyday, except perhaps "umbrellas", which is a good word to look at closely. To notice: a capital letter at the start of every line of a poem, and the rhyme of "tree" and "sea". Liking: ask whether the child enjoys the picture of rain falling in different places. A good first passage.

2. Aesop, "The Hare and the Tortoise"

Slow but steady wins the race.

The moral that ends the fable, in George Fyler Townsend's translation. Length: six words, a perfect single line. Words: easy. To notice: it is a complete sentence with no "I" or "you" in it, and it makes a claim you can argue about. Liking: read the fable first and ask whether the child wants to copy its lesson. Excellent for a child who finds handwriting hard: short, finished, and done well.

3. Stevenson, "Bed in Summer" (1885)

In winter I get up at night
And dress by yellow candle-light.

Also from A Child's Garden of Verses. Length: two lines. Words: easy, with one hyphenated word, "candle-light". To notice: the hyphen, and that the poem's joke only works if you know winter mornings are dark. Liking: a possible choice for a child who knows the feeling of being sent to bed while it is still light. A good second week.

4. Anna Sewell, Black Beauty (1877)

The first place that I can well remember was a large pleasant meadow with a pond of clear water in it.

The opening sentence of the novel, which is told by the horse himself. Length: one long sentence, about twenty words: right for eight or nine and up. Words: all readable. To notice: the narrator is a horse, so "I" is the horse; the sentence has no commas, yet reads easily. Liking: offer it to a child interested in animal stories. A good bridge from poems to prose.

5. Kenneth Grahame, The Wind in the Willows (1908)

The Mole had been working very hard all the morning, spring-cleaning his little home.

The novel's first sentence. Length: one sentence with a comma: fine for nine and up. Words: readable, with a hyphenated compound. To notice: the comma that separates what he was doing from the extra detail. Liking: read the next sentence together if the child wants to know what happens next. A good first sentence with a comma.

6. Lewis Carroll, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865)

"Curiouser and curiouser!" cried Alice (she was so much surprised, that for the moment she quite forgot how to speak good English);

From chapter 2. Length: long, and it does not end with a full stop in the original. Words: mostly familiar, but "curiouser" is a playful, nonstandard way of saying "more curious". To notice: quotation marks, an exclamation mark, brackets and a semicolon, all in one line. Liking: offer it to a reader who enjoys Alice's unusual language. Best for ten to twelve: a whole punctuation lesson in one sentence, and a chance to talk about why Carroll broke the rules on purpose.

What the comparison shows

  • Short can be enough. The six-word Aesop line offers a manageable complete sentence for a child still building handwriting control. The Carroll passage offers more punctuation to study, but it is also much longer.
  • Rhyme gives you something to notice. Stevenson's lines have a clear rhyme. After reading them aloud, the child can try looking at a word, picturing it, and writing it from memory.
  • Punctuation is a reason to move on. When a child copies rhymes easily, a passage with a comma (Grahame) and then one with speech marks (Carroll) gives them something new to notice.
  • Ask the child. If two passages pass the first three checks, let the child choose between them.

A simple plan for a month

WeekPassage typeExample from above
1A two-line rhymeStevenson, "Rain"
2A short sayingAesop, "Slow but steady wins the race."
3One plain sentence of proseBlack Beauty, opening
4A sentence with a comma or speech marksThe Wind in the Willows or Alice

Ten minutes a day is plenty. Keep the best line of each week in a small notebook; at the end of the month, the child has a book of their own.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good copywork passage for kids?

One the child can copy carefully in about ten minutes, made of words they can read, with one thing worth noticing, and ideally one they chose. A two-line rhyme or a short proverb is a good start.

How long should copywork be for an 8-year-old?

Short: often one or two lines, or a single sentence, done well. Quality matters more than quantity.

Should kids copy poems or prose?

Both. A short rhyme gives the child a pattern to notice; a prose sentence may offer a different kind of pattern, such as an interesting verb or a comma. Choose according to the child's reading and handwriting, rather than treating poetry and prose as fixed difficulty levels.

Where can I find copywork passages for free?

Public-domain classics are a rich source: Project Gutenberg has the full texts of all six works quoted here. Check each line against a reliable text before you copy it out.
