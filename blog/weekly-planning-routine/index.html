How to Use a Planner: A 15-Minute Sunday Evening Routine

How to use a planner so it lasts past February: one Sunday evening, fifteen minutes, minute by minute, and how to restart after a missed week.

Inkpot Lane · 10 September 2026 · 6 min read

Picture a Sunday evening. The dishes are done, the house has gone quiet, and your phone is already lit up with Monday: a meeting moved, a bill due, a message you still haven't answered. Wednesday looks like trouble. Somewhere on a shelf is a planner you bought with good intentions. You know, roughly, how to use a planner. What nobody showed you is a way of using one that survives past February. Here is what the next fifteen minutes can look like.

The short answer: plan once a week, for about fifteen minutes. Look at your big goal, check the quarter, choose three big rocks, add small ten-minute steps, give each day one top priority and set up a few habits. Close the week with your wins and one line of gratitude. If you miss a week, start again from today.

Why the last planner went quiet

Before you start, it helps to know what went wrong last time, because it probably wasn't you.

Most planners go quiet for three ordinary reasons. You tried to fill every box, so planning turned into homework. You planned the days but never looked at the bigger goal, so the weeks went by without adding up to anything. Or you missed a week, felt guilty and never opened the book again.

A good routine fixes all three. It's short, it links the week to the year, and it has a way back in built into it.

How to use a planner: the Sunday evening, minute by minute

It's half past seven. You make a cup of something warm and sit somewhere that isn't your desk. You set a timer for fifteen minutes. Your phone goes face down. Then you take it one step at a time.

Before the timer: two minutes on the week behind you

You look back first, and briefly. You write down the week's wins, including the small ones, because small wins are the ones that add up. You add one line of gratitude. You note how your body and mind held up: sleep, energy, mood, a word or two each. Then you empty your head onto the page, the brain dump, so the rattling list of things to remember stops rattling.

Last of all, you write the one thing you want next week to be about. It will feed straight into what comes next.

Minutes 1 to 2: look at the big picture

You turn to wherever your big goal for the year lives. It might be a vision board, a single sentence, or a 9-grid goal chart. You don't edit it. You just look at it. Two minutes of remembering why makes the other thirteen count.

Minutes 3 to 4: check the quarter

You look at your three goals for this quarter and mark each one that moved last week. You're honest about it. A goal that didn't move isn't a failure. It's information.

Minutes 5 to 7: choose three big rocks

You write the three things that would make this a good week if they happened. Each one comes from a quarter goal. Three, not ten. Big rocks go in the jar first, and the sand fits around them.

Minutes 8 to 10: add the ten-minute steps

For each goal you're working toward, you write a small step of about ten minutes. That is short enough to do on a tired Tuesday. These steps keep every goal alive, even the ones you aren't pushing hard this quarter.

Minutes 11 to 13: give every day one top priority

You glance across the week, meetings and all, and give each day one top priority. Only one. On a good day you'll do more. On a hard day, doing that one thing is enough. Wednesday, the one that looked like trouble, gets the smallest priority of the week. That's deliberate.

Minutes 14 to 15: set up your habits

You write the habits you're tracking this week, no more than three, and the smallest version of each for bad days. "Walk for twenty minutes" on a good day can be "put my shoes on and step outside" on a bad one.

The timer goes off. You close the book. The week is planned, and it's still only a quarter to eight.

Monday morning: one minute

Tomorrow, before you open your messages, you open the planner and read today's top priority. You do it first if you possibly can. You tick yesterday's habits while you're there. That takes about a minute, and it's the whole daily routine.

When you miss a week, and you will

Now picture a different Sunday, a few weeks from now. Last weekend went to a family visit and you didn't plan at all. The planner has been sitting on the shelf, and it's starting to feel like a reproach.

This part matters most:

  1. Don't go back and fill in the missed week. It's gone. Let it go.
  2. Turn to a fresh page and write today's date.
  3. Do the routine as normal, even if it's Wednesday.
  4. In your wins, write "I came back." It counts.

If your planner has dates printed in it, a missed week leaves a blank spread behind. If that is what made you give up before, it's worth reading our comparison of dated vs undated planners before you choose the next one.

What has changed by a quarter to eight

Look at what fifteen minutes did. Wednesday still has its meetings, but it also has one clear priority, and it's a small one. The goal you wrote in January is on your mind again. There are three rocks for the week, a handful of ten-minute steps, and three habits with a bad-day version of each. Your head is lighter because the brain dump is on paper.

Nothing dramatic happened. That's the point. A routine this short gets done on ordinary Sundays, and ordinary Sundays are most of the year.

Once a month, the same idea stretches to twenty minutes: see where each goal honestly stands, and pick the one thing that would make the biggest difference next month. Once a quarter, choose which goals to push, which to keep ticking over and which to pause.

Frequently asked questions

How do you use a planner effectively?

Plan once a week for about fifteen minutes: look at your big goal, check your quarter goals, choose three big rocks, add small steps and give each day one top priority. Then spend a minute each morning on that day's priority.

How long should weekly planning take?

About fifteen minutes. Any longer and it becomes a chore you'll skip. Any shorter and you won't connect the week to your goals.

What should I write in my planner every day?

One top priority, plus a tick for each habit you're tracking. That's enough for most days.

What if I miss a week in my planner?

Skip it and start fresh on today's date. Don't go back and fill in the missed week. Coming back is the habit that matters.
