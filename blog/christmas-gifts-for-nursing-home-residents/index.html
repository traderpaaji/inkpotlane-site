Christmas Gifts for Nursing Home Residents: A Checklist

Christmas gifts for nursing home residents, checked the way the room works: five questions to ask the home, then gifts that fit and survive the laundry.

Inkpot Lane · 10 November 2026 · 7 min read

A Christmas present for someone in a nursing home has to suit two things: the person receiving it, and the space where they now live. A favorite hobby might call for a book or a deck of cards; a new cardigan might need a name label and a fabric that can survive the home's laundry. Good Christmas gifts for nursing home residents begin with what that person enjoys, then take account of the practical details. This checklist helps you do both.

The short answer: Before you buy, ask the home five things: how much space there is, how clothes are labeled and laundered, what food is allowed, what electrical or scented items are permitted, and how gifts and visits work over Christmas. Then check four things: does it fit the space they have, does it need a name label, can they enjoy it the way they like (alone or with help they welcome), and is any setup or upkeep manageable? Time, visits and letters count as gifts too.

Why a room changes the gift

Residents of nursing homes in the United States that take part in Medicare or Medicaid have a federal right to keep and use their own possessions, including furniture and clothing, "as space permits," as long as that does not harm the rights, health or safety of other residents. The home, for its part, must take reasonable care to protect a resident's property from loss or theft.

Those two lines from the federal rules explain most of what makes a good gift. As space permits: the gift has to fit. Loss or theft: in a building where laundry, cleaning and care staff come and go, anything valuable or unmarked can go missing without anyone meaning harm. Homes outside the United States work under their own rules, but the same practical questions apply almost everywhere.

Christmas gifts for nursing home residents: ask the home first

A quick call to the home's activities coordinator, or another staff member they suggest, can save you buying something unsuitable. Ask:

  1. How much space is there? A private room and a shared room are very different. If the room is shared, ask which storage space is the resident's.
  2. How are clothes labeled and laundered? Some homes wash residents' clothes together and ask families to mark every item with the resident's name. Ask how they want it done, and whether anything must be washable at high temperatures.
  3. Is there a diet or food rule? Chocolate and biscuits are classic gifts, but a resident may be on a special diet, and some homes ask that food be handed to staff or kept in a sealed tin.
  4. What about electrical and scented items? Candles may not be allowed at all, and some homes check plug-in items or ask families to avoid strong scents in shared rooms. Ask before you buy a lamp, a heater or a diffuser.
  5. How do gifts and visits work over Christmas? Ask about holiday celebrations, where to leave parcels, and arranging a visit that suits the resident. In US nursing homes covered by the federal rules, residents have the right to receive visitors of their choosing at times of their choosing, subject to other residents' rights and reasonable clinical and safety limits.

The four checks every gift has to pass

Once you know the room, run each idea past four checks. A gift that fails one is not ruled out, but it needs a plan.

  • It fits. On the bedside table, on a shelf, or folded in a drawer. If it needs a wall, ask first.
  • It can be identified. Ask which belongings should carry the resident's name, especially clothing and anything that goes to the laundry, and label those the way the home prefers before you wrap them.
  • They can enjoy it. Think about how the person likes to do things, alone or with company. Make packaging easy to open, and offer help with any setup if they would like it.
  • Its upkeep is manageable. If a gift needs batteries, charging, passwords or a subscription, check that the resident can manage those or has someone happy to help. Paper puzzles need no charging or account.

Gifts that pass, sorted by what they are for

For the hands and the evenings

If the resident enjoys puzzles, games or drawing, a small activity they can come back to may be a thoughtful gift: a puzzle book with print large enough to read in the light of the room, a pack of playing cards with big pips, a jigsaw with large pieces, a sketch pad and soft pencils. Choose by what they enjoyed before, not by what is sold for "seniors." A crossword lover wants crosswords, not a coloring book.

For the eyes

Print size matters more than people expect. The US National Library Service for the Blind and Print Disabled puts typical adult books at 10 to 12 point type, with large-print materials commonly at 16 to 18 point. "Large print" on a cover is not one fixed size, so look for a book that states its type size, or ask to see a page. Our guide to gifts for seniors with low vision goes further into lighting, contrast and type.

For warmth

Bed socks with grips, a lap blanket in a washable fabric, a cardigan that opens at the front. All of them labeled. Choose easy-care fabrics that survive a hot wash, and avoid anything that needs dry cleaning.

For keeping in touch

A box of Christmas cards already stamped and addressed to the people they would want to write to. A small photo album with names written under the faces. If they use a calendar, choose one with the family birthdays already marked and help hang it somewhere easy to see.

Time

A planned visit can be a gift in itself. Put a date in the diary for January, when the Christmas visitors have gone home, and write it on a card inside the present: "I'm coming on the 9th. We'll do the crossword." That card may be the thing they keep.

What to leave at home

  • Valuables: good jewelry, cash, expensive watches. They can go missing in a busy building, through no one's fault.
  • Bulky things: big hampers, oversized plants and large ornaments may be hard to fit in. Check the space first.
  • Anything with a flame or a strong scent, unless the home has said yes.
  • Food you have not cleared with staff.
  • Clothes without a name in them. Mark them before you wrap them.

Frequently asked questions

What are good Christmas gifts for nursing home residents?

Something that suits the resident's interests and how they like to spend their time: a large-print puzzle book, labeled socks or a lap blanket, stamped Christmas cards, a photo album with names written in, or a calendar. A gift can be enjoyed alone or with help they welcome. A planned January visit, written on a card, is another thoughtful option.

What should you not give someone in a nursing home?

Valuables, bulky items, anything with a flame or a strong scent, food that has not been cleared with staff, and clothes without a name label. Ask the home about its own rules before you buy.

Should I label gifts for a nursing home resident?

Yes, especially clothing, blankets and anything that goes to the laundry. Some homes wash residents' clothes together and ask families to mark each item with the resident's name.

Are large-print books a good gift for nursing home residents?

Often, if the person enjoys reading or puzzles. Check the stated type size: typical books are 10 to 12 point, and large print is commonly 16 to 18 point. Seeing a sample page is the best test.
