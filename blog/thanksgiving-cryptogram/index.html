Thanksgiving Cryptogram: Three Free Puzzles With Answers

A Thanksgiving cryptogram to pass around the table: three free gratitude puzzles, one solved step by step, a simple table game, and every answer.

Inkpot Lane · 12 November 2026 · 8 min read

There is a quiet stretch on Thanksgiving afternoon that nobody plans for. The plates are stacked, the pie is still an hour away, and the people who cooked have finally sat down. Somebody reaches for a phone. That lull is the moment a Thanksgiving cryptogram suits best: one short line in code, a pencil, and a table of people who each spot a different word. It needs no board and no batteries, it is short enough to finish before the pie, and the answer, when it comes, is a line about being thankful.

The short answer: A cryptogram is a sentence in which every letter has been swapped for another, with the same swap used all the way through. Below are three free ones on the theme of gratitude. Each gives you two letters to start. The first is solved step by step, the other two are left for you, and all three answers are near the end of the page.

Thanksgiving cryptogram 1: the one we solve together

The rules fit in a breath. Each code letter stands for one real letter, and it stands for that same letter everywhere in the puzzle. In these three puzzles no letter ever stands for itself. The spaces and the punctuation are real, so you can see where each word begins and ends.

OKP HFKUI PAL YNBAWN HFN VKP OGQCO KZKP.

Two letters are given. In this puzzle the code letter A stands for O, and the code letter O stands for S. Write those in wherever they appear, and put a blank for everything else:

S _ _ / _ _ _ _ _ / _ O _ / _ _ _ O _ _ / _ _ _ / _ _ _ / S _ _ _ S / _ _ _ _.

Step 1: find THE. The code word HFN has three letters, and the same two letters, HF, begin the longer word HFKUI. THE is the most common word in written English, and words that begin with TH are everywhere, so try it: H is T, F is H, N is E.

S _ _ / T H _ _ _ / _ O _ / _ E _ O _ E / T H E / _ _ _ / S _ _ _ S / _ _ _ _.

Step 2: follow a pair. The code letters K and P sit side by side at the end of three words: OKP, VKP and KZKP. The first of those is S, blank, blank, and it opens the sentence. Try SAY, which makes K stand for A and P stand for Y.

S A Y / T H A _ _ / Y O _ / _ E _ O _ E / T H E / _ A Y / S _ _ _ S / A _ A Y.

Step 3: let the sentence help. SAY, then T H A and two blanks, then Y O and one blank. That is SAY THANK YOU. Three more letters fall: L is U, U is N, I is K.

Step 4: try a word, then check it. The six-letter word now reads blank E blank O blank E, and it comes just before THE. Try BEFORE. It fits, and it gives three more letters: Y is B, B is F and W is R. The next word reads blank A Y, and in this sentence DAY makes sense, so V is D.

S A Y / T H A N K / Y O U / B E F O R E / T H E / D A Y / S _ _ _ S / A _ A Y.

Two words are left. A, blank, A, Y is AWAY. And what does a day do when you are not watching? It slips away, and SLIPS fits S, blank, blank, blank, S. Both words fit the sense, and neither one clashes with a letter you have already found.

SAY THANK YOU BEFORE THE DAY SLIPS AWAY.

Notice what did the work. Not one guess was made from nothing: a three-letter word, a pair of letters that repeated, and then the meaning of the sentence itself. Those are the same three tools you need for the next two.

Two more Thanksgiving cryptograms to solve yourself

Each of these uses its own code, so nothing you learned about the letters in puzzle 1 carries over. Only the method does.

Puzzle 2. Given: the code letter C stands for N, and the code letter N stands for D.

CMSTVW SDW UMMN, ECN SDW UMMN LWWPL SM UOMI.

A nudge if you want one: one three-letter word appears twice.

Puzzle 3. Given: the code letter C stands for H, and the code letter H stands for R.

N LHNSDRIM CDNHS RKPEB LKRSB KP UHEKPNHG CUIHB.

A nudge: it opens with a one-letter word, and in English a one-letter word is almost always A or I.

How to turn one into a Thanksgiving table game

A cryptogram is usually solved alone. It works better than you might expect with six people and one sheet of paper.

  1. Copy one puzzle in big letters onto a sheet of paper, with a blank under every code letter. Put it where everyone can see it.
  2. Read the given letters aloud and let the youngest person write them in.
  3. Go round the table. Each person offers one word and says why: "that three-letter word shows up twice, so I think it is THE." A guess with a reason is worth more than a lucky one.
  4. Use pencil. A wrong guess is no failure; rub it out and the puzzle is exactly where it was.
  5. Let whoever cracks the last word read the line aloud.

If the television is on in the next room, the seven groups of the National Dog Show make a second game for the same table.

For young children, give more help before you start: write in all of one vowel, or the whole first word. The puzzle should feel like a door that is already a little open. A child who enjoys this may like making codes as much as breaking them, and secret codes for kids has several to write by hand.

Make your own thank-you in code

This is the part that turns a puzzle into a keepsake. Each person writes one thank-you line for someone else at the table, in code.

  1. Write the alphabet, A to Z, along one line.
  2. Under it, write the 26 letters again in a jumbled order. Check that no letter sits under itself and that you have used every letter once.
  3. Write your thank-you sentence. Five to nine words is about right, and plain words are best: "Thank you for teaching me to drive."
  4. Swap each letter for the one beneath it. Keep the spaces and the punctuation.
  5. Give two letters free, and hand it over.

One caution: check your coding once before you pass it on. A single wrong letter can stop a solver cold, and they will blame themselves, not you.

Frequently asked questions

What is a Thanksgiving cryptogram?

It is an ordinary cryptogram, a sentence in which each letter has been swapped for another, whose hidden line suits the day. The three on this page each hide a short saying about gratitude.

Where are the answers to these Thanksgiving cryptograms?

All three answers are on this page, under "Answers to the three puzzles," just above these questions. Puzzle 1 is also worked through step by step.

Can children solve these cryptograms?

A child who reads comfortably can solve them with help. Give extra letters before starting, such as every E or the whole first word, and work on it together. For younger children, making a code of their own is often more fun than breaking one.

Do I need to know the saying to solve it?

No. You do not need to recognize the saying first. You solve it from short words, repeated letters and the meaning that begins to show, just as in the worked example.

What makes a cryptogram easier or harder?

The number of letters given at the start, the length of the line, and how many short words it has. A line with THE, AND or a one-letter word gives you a way in. A long line with no given letters and few short words is the hard end.
