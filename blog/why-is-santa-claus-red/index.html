Why Is Santa Claus Red? What the Old Pictures Show

Why is Santa Claus red? Not because of a soft drink. Follow his coat through 1823, Thomas Nast, the Puck covers and 1931 to see who really chose the color.

Inkpot Lane · 12 November 2026 · 6 min read

Open the 1823 poem that most of us still read on Christmas Eve and look for Santa's coat. It is not there. The poem gives us his belly, his dimples, his cherry nose and his clothes "all in fur, from his head to his foot," with soot on them for good measure. It never says what color the fur was. So why is Santa Claus red? The popular answer is that a soft drink company chose the color in the 1930s. The pictures say otherwise, and they say it in a long, slow, well-dated way that is more interesting than the myth.

The short answer: Santa Claus was not made red by an advertisement. The 1823 poem "A Visit from St. Nicholas" names no color for his clothes. The cartoonist Thomas Nast drew him in Harper's Weekly for about thirty years, and Coca-Cola's own history says Nast changed the coat from tan to red over that time. By the early 1900s magazine covers showed him in red and white. Haddon Sundblom's Coca-Cola paintings, which began in 1931, did not invent the red coat; they made one warm, grandfatherly version of him familiar everywhere.

The poem leaves the color to you

On 23 December 1823 a newspaper in Troy, New York, printed fifty-six anonymous lines about a visit from St. Nicholas. The poem is the source of much of what we picture today: the sleigh, the eight reindeer, the chimney, the finger laid beside the nose. For his clothing it says only that he was dressed all in fur. What the poem does color is his face. His cheeks are like roses and his nose like a cherry, and that may be where some of the "rosy" picture comes from.

That silence is useful. The words leave an artist free, and for most of the next century artists did what they liked. Wikipedia's article on the Santa suit says that before Thomas Nast's work, Santa's outfit was tan, and that Nast also drew a green suit at some point.

Thomas Nast: thirty years of drawing him

Thomas Nast, a cartoonist for Harper's Weekly, drew Santa Claus for about thirty years. Wikipedia's page on Santa Claus dates an early Nast Santa to the issue of 3 January 1863, in which he wears an American flag and supports the Union cause. Some accounts give 1862 for the same drawing. Either way, it is the Civil War, and this Santa is a small elflike figure, not yet the one in the shopping mall.

Coca-Cola's company history, which has every reason to claim the credit and does not, says Nast changed Santa's coat from tan to red over those thirty years. By 1881, Wikipedia's Santa suit page shows a Nast drawing with Santa in what we would call the modern suit.

Magazine covers fix the look

Wikipedia's Santa Claus article says that in the first few years of the twentieth century Santa appeared on several covers of the humor magazine Puck in red and white, in essentially the form we know; it shows a December 1905 cover. Other advertisers had used him by then, too. The same article records a White Rock mineral water Santa in 1915 and color Santa images in drink-mixer advertisements in 1923 to 1925.

So by the time any soft drink company asked an artist to paint a Santa, the red coat was already the standard.

1931: what the Coca-Cola Santa really did

The Coca-Cola Company's history page tells it straight. Its Christmas advertising began in the 1920s with shopping-themed magazine ads, and the first Santa ads showed a stern Claus in the style of Thomas Nast. In 1930 an artist named Fred Mizen painted a department-store Santa drinking a Coke. Then, in 1931, Haddon Sundblom's first painting appeared in The Saturday Evening Post. Sundblom drew on Clement Clarke Moore's poem for a warm, friendly, plump and human Santa. The company says plainly that Santa wore a red coat before Sundblom painted him.

His Santas ran in Coca-Cola advertising from 1931 to 1964, and for decades afterward the company's ads used images based on his originals. Wikipedia sums up the effect: Sundblom standardized the form of the Santa that Nast and others had already drawn. That is a real achievement. It is not an invention.

Why red won out is a question the sources used here do not answer. St. Nicholas was a bishop, and Wikipedia's Santa suit page observes that European versions of the figure still wear clothing like a bishop's. That is a suggestion, not a finding.

A good question for a child with a crayon

Here is the practical lesson, and it is a nice one for a Christmas Eve table. If Santa's coat was open to every artist for a hundred years, it is open to yours. Hand a child the line from the poem about the fur and ask what color it is. Some will say red because that is what they have seen. Others will say brown, as bear fur is, or gray, or white. All of them are following the poem exactly.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Santa Claus red?

No source settles the exact reason. What the record shows is a gradual change: Thomas Nast moved his coat from tan toward red over about thirty years, and by the early 1900s magazine covers showed him in red and white.

Did Coca-Cola make Santa red?

No. Coca-Cola's own history says Santa wore a red coat before Haddon Sundblom painted him for its ads in 1931. Sundblom's paintings made one friendly, plump version of Santa familiar to millions.

What color was Santa before red?

Wikipedia's Santa suit page says his outfit was tan before Nast, and that Nast also drew a green suit.

What does "A Visit from St. Nicholas" say Santa wears?

It says he is dressed all in fur, from his head to his foot, with clothes tarnished with ashes and soot. It gives no color for the clothes, only roses for his cheeks and a cherry for his nose.
