Christmas Puzzles for Adults: Three to Solve Right Here

Try three Christmas puzzles for adults: a hidden-message word search, a four-clue logic grid and a festive suspect mystery, with answers and explanations.

Inkpot Lane · 8 October 2026 · 8 min read

Christmas puzzles for adults don't usually come from a spy agency. In December 2015, though, the Director of GCHQ, Britain's signals intelligence agency, sent out his Christmas card with a puzzle printed inside: a grid of squares to shade black or white. Shade it correctly and the grid became a QR code, which led to a second stage of puzzles, and then more. GCHQ put the puzzle online too, and people all over the world joined in.

By 28 January 2016, GCHQ said, nearly 600,000 people had finished that first grid, and about 30,000 had reached the final stage. With entries closing on 31 January, nobody had yet fully completed it.

The short answer: Good Christmas puzzles for adults come in different forms, from word searches to logic grids and elimination mysteries. Try the three below: a hidden-message word search, a four-clue logic grid and a small suspect mystery. Each has one answer you can check, and the answers and reasoning are at the end.

Why a mix of Christmas puzzles works

GCHQ's first grid was a way into a much larger challenge. Hundreds of thousands of people finished it, and far fewer reached the final stage. The numbers can't tell us why people joined in or how much they enjoyed it. What makes the challenge interesting is how it was built: one finished puzzle opened the door to the next.

A Christmas puzzle table can offer that same variety, even when the puzzles aren't linked. People around it may want different things. One person wants a quiet word search with a cup of tea. Another wants a chain of clues to follow, and someone else likes working through a long list until one name is left. A short puzzle offers an approachable start, while a longer one gives the keen solver something to come back to.

So the three puzzles below each use a different kind of thinking: spotting hidden words, matching people to gifts and ruling out suspects. Each one is original and small enough to finish in a few minutes. Have a pencil ready.

Puzzle one: a word search with a secret

A word search is an easy place to begin. The words are already listed, and your job is to find where they sit in the grid. A hidden message adds a reason to finish. When every word is circled, the letters left over spell something.

Find STAR, NOEL, SNOW and GIFT. Every word runs across or down, and two squares are shared by more than one word. When all four are circled, six letters are left. Read them left to right, top to bottom, and you have the message.

You can work at your own pace, and each word you find makes the rest of the search a little shorter.

Puzzle two: a four-clue logic grid

A logic grid asks for more. Nothing is hidden in plain sight. Instead, a handful of clues lets you work out who goes with what, and each clue rules something out. Your job is to keep track until only one answer is left in every row.

Three neighbors, Mabel, Otis and Ruth, have each wrapped one gift: a scarf, a jigsaw or a teapot. Each used a different paper: red, green or gold. Who wrapped what, in which paper?

  1. The scarf is wrapped in red paper.
  2. Ruth's gift is in neither red nor gold paper.
  3. The teapot is not in green paper.
  4. Otis did not use gold paper.

Put an X in a square when a clue rules it out, and a tick when only one square is left in its row or column. Clue 2 gives you your first tick. After that, every tick tells you something about the other two blocks of the grid. You never need to guess: every square can be settled by reasoning alone, and all four clues are needed. If this is your first grid, our guide on how to solve logic grid puzzles walks through the marks step by step.

Puzzle three: a suspect list to cross out

This time, instead of filling a grid, you cross names off a list. Each clue removes the suspects who can't fit it. Work through the clues in order until one name is left.

Every clue is true about the culprit, and every clue rules out at least one person. Read the times exactly: "before 4:30" means 4:29 or earlier. If you are counting as you go, you should have three suspects left after clue 2.

Eight rows make a quick introduction. Longer versions can run to hundreds of names, or more than a thousand, and that's when careful tracking really matters.

How to choose Christmas puzzles for adults

If those three were fun, here is what to look for when you want more than a few minutes' worth.

  • One answer you can prove. In a well-made logic or elimination puzzle, the clues lead to one answer without a guess.
  • Solutions you can use. A word search needs a clear answer grid. A harder logic puzzle is better with worked explanations that show how the answer follows from the clues.
  • A real climb. The first puzzles in a book should teach you the moves, and the later ones should ask for all of them at once.
  • Print you can read. Large print matters for word searches most of all, where small letters can swim together.
  • Something to follow. A story running through the puzzles gives you a reason to start the next one.

Frequently asked questions

What are good Christmas puzzles for adults?

Word searches with a hidden message, logic grids and elimination mysteries are three good kinds. They ask for different things: patience, careful reasoning and keeping track of a long list. A mix suits a family table, because everyone can find a way in.

Are there Christmas puzzles for adults with answers?

Yes. All three puzzles on this page have their answers and explanations in the Answers section. When you choose a puzzle book, look for a clear answer key and, for logic puzzles, worked solutions that show the steps.

What was the GCHQ Christmas puzzle?

In December 2015 the Director of GCHQ, the UK's intelligence and security agency, put a grid-shading puzzle in his Christmas card. Solving it made a QR code that led to harder stages. GCHQ said nearly 600,000 people finished the first stage and about 30,000 reached the final one.

Which Christmas puzzle is best for a group?

A word search or a small logic grid. Both can be shared on one page, and nobody needs any special knowledge. Save the long elimination cases for one keen solver by the fire.
