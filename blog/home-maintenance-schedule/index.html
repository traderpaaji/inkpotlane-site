Home Maintenance Schedule: Monthly, Seasonal, Yearly

A home maintenance schedule built from official safety advice: what to check monthly, each spring and fall, yearly and every ten years, and how to track it.

Inkpot Lane · 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

Home maintenance can slip by one forgotten job at a time. A filter that was due in spring is still in the furnace in December. A smoke alarm that came with the house turns out, when someone finally looks at the label, to be older than the children. Jobs like these can be overlooked when no reminder has been set. A home maintenance schedule is nothing more than that: a date for each job, so the house stops depending on someone remembering.

The short answer: Give regular jobs five rhythms. Every dryer load: clean the lint filter, before and after the cycle. Monthly: test smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, and inspect the filters in central heating and cooling equipment. Spring and fall: a pre-season cooling check-up in spring and a heating check-up in fall. Every year: replace alarm batteries (unless they are sealed long-life batteries) and clean lint out of the dryer vent pipe. About every ten years: check each smoke alarm's manufacture date for replacement. Everything else, from water heaters to gutters, follows your own manuals and your own house.

This schedule starts from the guidance of three U.S. sources: the U.S. Fire Administration, the Consumer Product Safety Commission and ENERGY STAR. Where a job depends on your appliance, your climate, local rules or your house, we say so and point you to the manual or a qualified professional rather than inventing a number.

Why a home maintenance schedule beats a checklist

A checklist tells you what to do. A schedule tells you when. The difference matters because regular checks keep small jobs from being overlooked, and a written schedule makes it easier to book the jobs that need professional help. Putting each job into a rhythm, monthly, seasonal, yearly or once a decade, means you only ever have to think about this month's short list.

Monthly

  • Test every smoke alarm. The U.S. Fire Administration recommends testing smoke alarms every month, whatever their power source. Follow the maker's instructions for your model.
  • Test carbon monoxide alarms. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends testing CO alarms monthly too, and says CO alarms belong on each level of the home and outside sleeping areas.
  • Inspect the heating and cooling filters. ENERGY STAR recommends inspecting, cleaning or changing the air filters in a central air conditioner, furnace or heat pump once a month. A monthly check does not necessarily mean a monthly replacement; your equipment's instructions and the state of the filter decide that.

Every time you dry a load

  • Clean the dryer's lint filter. The U.S. Fire Administration's clothes dryer safety flyer advises cleaning the lint filter before and after each cycle and never running the dryer without it, and notes that skipping lint filter and vent cleaning raises the risk of a dryer fire.

Each spring

  • Have the cooling system checked. ENERGY STAR recommends an annual pre-season check-up by a contractor, with the cooling system checked in spring and the heating system in fall.
  • Include the condensate drain in that check-up. ENERGY STAR lists inspecting the condensate drain of a central air conditioner, furnace or heat pump in cooling mode among the items of a maintenance visit.
  • Look behind the dryer. The USFA flyer recommends cleaning lint from the back of the dryer, checking that the venting system behind it is not damaged, crushed or restricted, and making sure the outdoor vent cover opens when the dryer runs. Spring and fall are our suggested reminders for this, alongside the HVAC check; the flyer itself gives no interval.

Each fall

  • Have the heating system checked before the cold weather, as ENERGY STAR advises.
  • Check thermostat settings for the season, also on the ENERGY STAR list.
  • Repeat the dryer vent look.

Every year

  • Replace alarm batteries. The USFA recommends replacing the batteries in 9-volt smoke alarms, and the backup batteries in hardwired ones, at least once a year; the CPSC gives the same yearly advice for smoke and CO alarms. Alarms with sealed ten-year batteries are the exception: follow the maker's instructions.
  • Clean lint out of the dryer vent pipe. The CPSC recommends doing this at least once a year. A quick look behind the dryer does not replace it; follow your dryer's instructions, and use a professional if the vent run is long or hard to reach.
  • Pick one annual date for it, the same every year, so it is never a question of "when did we last do this?"

Every ten years

  • Replace smoke alarms. The USFA says smoke alarms need replacing ten years from their manufacture date, not from when they were installed. Look for the manufacture date on each alarm and write it down; for sealed long-life models, follow the maker's instructions.

Jobs your own house decides

Plenty of important jobs are missing from the lists above because the right timing depends on the house: flushing or servicing a water heater, clearing gutters, checking a sump pump, servicing a septic system, resealing a deck, chimney sweeping, pest checks. For each one:

  1. Find the manual or the installer's advice, and use their interval rather than a number from the internet.
  2. Write the interval next to the job, with the date you last did it.
  3. Add it to the right rhythm, so it comes up on its own.

If you are new to a house, the previous owner, the home inspection report or the companies who installed the appliances are the fastest sources of those intervals.

How to keep the schedule going

  • One place. Keep the schedule, the dates done and the appliance details together. A schedule on a calendar app and model numbers on a fridge door soon drift apart.
  • Write down what you did, not just that you did it. "Changed the furnace filter, 16 x 25 x 1, 3 March" is far more useful next time than a tick.
  • Note who you called. The plumber who fixed it well is worth remembering; so is the one who did not.

Frequently asked questions

What should be on a home maintenance schedule?

A useful starting schedule: monthly smoke and carbon monoxide alarm tests and filter checks; the dryer lint filter every load; a cooling check-up in spring and a heating check-up in fall; yearly alarm batteries and dryer vent cleaning; and smoke-alarm replacement ten years from the manufacture date. Then add the jobs your own appliances and house call for, using their manuals.

How often should I change my furnace filter?

ENERGY STAR says to inspect, clean or change filters once a month. How often the filter actually needs replacing depends on the filter and the system, so check the manual for yours.

How often should smoke alarms be replaced?

The U.S. Fire Administration says 10 years from the manufacture date shown on the alarm, and that alarms should be tested monthly.

When should I service my heating and air conditioning?

ENERGY STAR recommends an annual pre-season check-up: the cooling system in spring and the heating system in fall.

How do I remember home maintenance jobs?

Give every job a rhythm (monthly, seasonal, yearly or longer), keep the schedule and the record of what was done in one place, and write down the details each time.
