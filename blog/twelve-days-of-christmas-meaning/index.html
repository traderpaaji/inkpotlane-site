Twelve Days of Christmas Meaning: What the Record Shows

Twelve days of Christmas meaning: a London children's book of about 1780 printed it as a memory game. The secret-code story first appears in 1979.

Inkpot Lane · 12 November 2026 · 6 min read

Around 1780, a children's book was printed in London under the title Mirth Without Mischief. Among its pages was a rhyme in which each verse added one more gift to a list that the singer had to recite from memory, starting again at the beginning every time. A slip cost a forfeit. That, as far as anyone can show, is the first appearance in print of what we now call "The Twelve Days of Christmas." The question people type into a search box, the twelve days of Christmas meaning, usually has a bigger answer in mind. Here is what the record supports, and what it does not.

The short answer: The twelve days run from Christmas Day, 25 December, to 5 January, the eve of Epiphany. The song's earliest known printing is Mirth Without Mischief, London, about 1780, and nineteenth-century writers describe it as a memory and forfeits game. The popular claim that its gifts were a secret code for Catholic teaching is traced to a 1979 article by the hymnologist Hugh D. McKellar, which offered no evidence; the earliest source the references give is from 1979, centuries after the period the claim describes. The modern tune and the words "four calling birds" come from Frederic Austin's 1909 arrangement.

The twelve days are a real season

Before the song, there was the calendar. The twelve days of Christmas begin on Christmas Day and end on 5 January; the next day, 6 January, is Epiphany. Twelfth Night is the evening of 5 January.

A game, not a creed

The earliest printed text is a children's book, and the nineteenth-century collectors who wrote about the rhyme agree with that setting. Wikipedia's account cites James Halliwell (1842), Henry Husk (1864) and Lady Gomme (1898), all describing the same thing: players recite the verses in turn, and whoever makes a mistake pays a small penalty, a kiss or a trinket.

It is a very good memory game, because the load grows with every verse. On the first day the singer remembers one gift. By the twelfth, the list has twelve items, and the singer is repeating the entire sequence.

The sums are fun to check. On each day the true love sends one more kind of gift, in a larger number, and repeats all the earlier kinds. On day 3 that is 3 of one, 2 of another and 1 of the first: 6 in all. The daily counts run 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, 28, 36, 45, 55, 66 and 78, and they add up to 364 gifts, one short of a year of days.

Where did the gifts come from?

Nobody knows, and the scholars quoted in Wikipedia's article say so. Several suggestions have been made, and each is only a suggestion.

  • A French origin. Peter and Iona Opie, in their dictionary of nursery rhymes, noted that the red-legged partridge, which perches in trees, reached England only around 1770, so the first gift may have come from France. In 1916 Anne Gilchrist suggested that "pear tree" is a corruption of the French word for partridge. William and Ceil Baring-Gould thought the "French hens" might really be Breton hens.
  • A northern English home. Wikipedia also notes a northern English connection, particularly around Newcastle upon Tyne, where a broadsheet very close to the 1780 text was printed.
  • A birds puzzle. The fourth gift in the 1780 text is a word for black birds, "colly birds," not "calling birds." "Colly" means black. Variants such as "canary" and "curley" appeared later, and Frederic Austin's 1909 arrangement settled on "calling birds," which most people now sing.

The secret-code claim: when it appears

You may have heard that every gift stood for a point of Catholic doctrine, and that the song was a secret catechism for English Catholics at a time when practicing the faith was illegal, from 1558 to 1829. This is a story about a story, so it is worth putting its dates in order.

  • 1979: the Canadian hymnologist Hugh D. McKellar proposed it in an article titled "How to Decode the Twelve Days of Christmas." He offered no evidence, according to the reference used here.
  • 1982: Father Hal Stockert made a similar suggestion, in an article posted online in 1995.
  • 1987 and 1992: Father James Gilhooley, a college chaplain in New York, raised the idea again.

The article on Wikipedia also reports that the fact-checking site Snopes concluded the theory is incorrect, on the reasoning that none of the gifts would distinguish a Catholic from a Protestant, so there would have been nothing to hide. We did not open the Snopes page itself, so that is a report of a report. The sturdier point is the plain order of dates: the earliest source the references give for the code claim is 1979, and the earliest printed song is about two centuries older.

None of this is a judgment about faith. People of any belief can sing a song about birds, drummers and pipers, and anyone can find meaning in it. The question here is only whether the meaning was put there in the sixteenth century, and the sources used here do not show it was.

How to play it the way it began

This is the best answer to "what does it mean": it means play. Here is our adaptation of the original game for a table of any size.

  1. Sit in a circle. The first player names a gift, any gift.
  2. The next player repeats it and adds a gift of their own, and so on around the circle.
  3. Anyone who slips pays a forfeit, an old and cheerful idea: a rhyme, a joke, or a small task such as passing the salt without being asked.
  4. If the list gets too long, stop at six or seven and start a fresh round with a different player leading.

Children enjoy being allowed to make mistakes on purpose. Adults tend to enjoy the way the game exposes who actually listens.

Frequently asked questions

What is the meaning of the twelve days of Christmas?

The twelve days are the Christmas season, from 25 December to 5 January. The song is first printed in a London children's book of about 1780, where it is a memory and forfeits game. Later writers have proposed other meanings, but the sources used here show no evidence for them from before 1979.

Was The Twelve Days of Christmas a secret code?

The claim that it was a secret Catholic catechism first appears in a 1979 article by Hugh D. McKellar, which offered no evidence. Wikipedia reports that Snopes concluded the idea is incorrect. Earlier writers describe the song as a children's game.

How many gifts are given in total?

  1. Day one gives 1, day two gives 3 (counting the gifts again), and so on to 78 on day twelve; the twelve daily totals add up to 364.

What are the twelve days of Christmas?

From Christmas Day, 25 December, to 5 January. Twelfth Night is the evening of 5 January, and 6 January is Epiphany.
