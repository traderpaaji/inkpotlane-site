Christmas Eve Ghost Story: How the Custom Began

A Christmas Eve ghost story is an old habit: Shakespeare's winter tale, Dickens's 1843 carol, a joke from 1891 and M. R. James reading aloud. How to hold one.

Inkpot Lane · 12 November 2026 · 6 min read

By 1891 the Christmas ghost story was so well established that a humorist could make a book out of laughing at it. "It was Christmas Eve," Jerome K. Jerome begins in Told After Supper, and then he explains why: "It always is Christmas Eve, in a ghost story." He goes on to describe the ghosts' great gala night, when "everybody in Ghostland who IS anybody" comes out to show off their winding-sheets, and the living, in their turn, sit round the fire and tell each other about it. A joke only works if the audience already knows the habit. So where did the Christmas Eve ghost story come from, and how do you hold such an evening today?

The short answer: Nobody founded the custom; it grew. Shakespeare already has a boy say that a sad tale of sprites and goblins is best for winter. Charles Dickens published A Christmas Carol in December 1843 with the subtitle "Being a Ghost Story of Christmas," and magazines of his day imitated his Christmas numbers with ghost stories of their own. By 1891 Jerome was joking about it. The scholar M. R. James, according to Wikipedia, first read his ghost stories aloud as Christmas Eve entertainments, and his first collection came out in 1904.

A sad tale for winter

In The Winter's Tale, a young prince named Mamillius is asked by his mother to tell her a story. Happy or sad? He chooses sad: "A sad tale's best for winter. I have one / Of sprites and goblins." The lines come in Act 2, Scene 1 of Shakespeare's play (the Folger text is online). It is not a Christmas scene, but it names the idea at the center of the custom: long dark evenings make a good setting for a tale that is a little frightening, told to people who are safe.

1843: Dickens puts a ghost in the title

A Christmas Carol was published by Chapman and Hall on 19 December 1843. Its full title page reads "A Christmas Carol. In Prose. Being a Ghost Story of Christmas." The first printing of 6,000 copies, at five shillings each, sold out by Christmas Eve. Marley's ghost and three spirits are the engine of the whole story, and yet it is a story about generosity. That mixture, a fright that ends in warmth, is what later Christmas ghost stories are measured against.

Dickens also edited magazines, and his Christmas numbers set a pattern. The Spectator, in a feature on the history of the custom, says that storytelling at Christmas became a literary convention through Dickens and the periodicals of his time, and that many imitated his magazine Household Words with ghost-filled Christmas issues.

1891: the joke

Told After Supper appeared in 1891 from the Leadenhall Press. Jerome's introduction teases every part of the formula. The young man who is put to sleep in the west wing on Christmas Eve is always visited by a lady in her nightdress who sits on the bed, says nothing and vanishes, and who is explained at breakfast as the ghost of a woman murdered in that very bed (or one who murdered somebody there). The skeptical guest who boasts he will sleep in the haunted room comes down next morning with "snow-white hair." Jerome's complaint is that all such stories are alike, "but this of course is not our fault but the fault of ghosts, who never will try any new performances."

It is a gentle, affectionate satire, and the best evidence we have that the habit was already old.

M. R. James: the reading aloud

Montague Rhodes James, a Cambridge scholar who became Provost of King's College, Cambridge, in 1905 and Provost of Eton in 1918, wrote ghost stories that remain among the most admired of their kind. According to Wikipedia, he first read them aloud to friends and selected students as Christmas Eve entertainments, and many of the stories were written for such readings. His first collection, Ghost Stories of an Antiquary, was published in 1904. The Spectator notes that only one of his stories has a Christmas setting, so the habit was the occasion, not the subject.

What makes his example useful is the setting: a small room, a few listeners and one story read aloud.

How to hold a Christmas Eve ghost story evening

You do not need a haunted house. You need a room, a fire or a few candles, and one story chosen with care.

  1. Pick one story, about fifteen minutes long. That is long enough to settle a room and short enough to hold it.
  2. Read it yourself beforehand, aloud. Mark the pauses. The best moments are silences.
  3. Dim the room, but leave a light on in the hall. The aim is hush, not alarm.
  4. Choose for the youngest and the most nervous person present. A frightening story is a gift only to people who asked for one.
  5. End on the warm note. Dickens's lesson holds: a fright that ends in kindness is what people want to carry to bed.

For an evening shared with children, or with people who do not enjoy being frightened, the same room works for a different kind of tale.

Frequently asked questions

Why do people tell ghost stories at Christmas?

Nobody can name a single origin. Shakespeare has a character say that a sad winter tale of sprites and goblins is best; Dickens made a ghost story the center of his 1843 Christmas book; and by the 1890s the habit was familiar enough for Jerome K. Jerome to joke about it.

Is A Christmas Carol a ghost story?

Its own title page says so: "Being a Ghost Story of Christmas." It was published on 19 December 1843.

Which M. R. James story is set at Christmas?

According to The Spectator, only one of his stories has a Christmas setting: "The Story of a Disappearance and an Appearance."

Can a ghost story be told to children?

That depends on the child. Choose a gentle story, read it earlier in the evening, and stop if anyone is unhappy. Many families find a Christmas mystery to puzzle over a better fit.
