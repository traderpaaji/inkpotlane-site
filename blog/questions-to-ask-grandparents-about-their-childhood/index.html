Questions to Ask Grandparents About Their Childhood, by Topic

Questions to ask grandparents about their childhood, grouped from the kitchen table to Christmas morning, with five habits that turn an answer into a story.

Inkpot Lane · 14 November 2026 · 8 min read

On 23 October 2003, a small recording booth opened in Grand Central Terminal in New York, and the oral historian Studs Terkel cut the ribbon. The idea behind it, called StoryCorps, was simple: two people who know each other sit down for forty minutes, one asks questions, the other answers, and, with their permission, a copy of the conversation goes to the American Folklife Center at the Library of Congress. In 2015 StoryCorps asked high school students to do the same thing at home over the Thanksgiving weekend, interviewing a grandparent or another elder. Every one of those conversations needed a first question. If you are looking for questions to ask grandparents about their childhood, this guide gives you the questions and, just as useful, a way to ask them.

The short answer: The best questions are small and specific. "What was your childhood like?" gets a summary; "What was on the kitchen table at breakfast?" gets a scene, and a scene turns into a story. Start with an object or a place, ask for one particular day, leave room for silence, follow whatever surprises you, and write down or record what you hear. The questions below are grouped from home to holidays so you can pick a theme for each visit.

Why small questions work better than big ones

A big question asks a person to sum up a decade, and most of us cannot do that on the spot. A small one asks for a single picture: a stove, a sound, a walk to school. It is far easier to describe one picture than to sum up ten years, and one picture tends to lead to the next. That is the whole method in one line: ask about things, and the people will follow.

It also helps to remember what kind of conversation this is. It is not a test, and there are no wrong answers. If a grandparent says "I don't remember," the kindest next question is usually a smaller one, or a different subject altogether.

Five habits that turn an answer into a story

  1. Start with a thing, not a year. "What did your mother cook on Sundays?" works better than "What were the 1950s like?"
  2. Ask for one day. "Tell me about one Christmas morning you remember" gets further than "What were Christmases like?"
  3. Let the quiet do its work. Count to five before you fill a pause. The best part of an answer often comes after it.
  4. Follow the surprise. If they mention an uncle you have never heard of, the list can wait.
  5. Keep it. Write it down that evening, or ask before the visit whether you may record it on your phone.

Home and the kitchen table

  • What did your kitchen look like? Where did everyone sit?
  • What was a normal breakfast in your house?
  • Who did the cooking, and what was the dish everyone asked for?
  • What did your house smell like on a baking day?
  • Was there a radio or a television? Where did it sit, and who chose what was on?
  • What job did you have around the house, and did you ever get out of it?

Neighbors, the street and the telephone

  • Who lived next door, and what were they like?
  • Did your family have a telephone? Was it a party line, shared with the neighbors?
  • How did you know if a call was for your house, and did anyone listen in?
  • Did anything come to the door: milk, bread, the mail, a salesman?
  • Where did children play on your street, and what games did you play?

A party line was a telephone connection shared by several households. Some systems used distinctive ringing patterns that everyone on the line could hear; others rang only selected phones. Conversations had little privacy, because another subscriber could pick up and listen. Party lines were common in the United States in the middle of the twentieth century and had mostly gone by the late 1980s, so for many grandparents this question opens a whole drawer of stories.

School and growing up

  • How did you get to school, and who did you walk with?
  • What was your classroom like? Do you remember your teacher's name?
  • What did you do at recess?
  • What was the trouble you got into that you still smile about?
  • What did you want to be when you grew up?

Money, first jobs and the corner store

  • What could you buy with a nickel or a dime?
  • What was your first job, and what did you do with the first money you earned?
  • Where did your family shop, and was there a store where everyone knew your name?
  • What was the first big thing your family bought that you remember arriving?

Summers and Saturdays

  • What did a summer day look like when school let out?
  • Where did you go on a Saturday afternoon?
  • Did your family ever take a road trip? Who sat where in the car?
  • What did you do on a hot evening before air conditioning, if your house had none?
  • Was there a fair, a parade or a swimming hole you went back to every year?

Winter, holidays and Christmas mornings

  • What happened on a snow day?
  • How did your family choose and decorate the tree, if you had one?
  • What was one present you hoped for, and did you get it?
  • What did your family eat on the big holiday of the year?
  • Who came to visit, and who was always last to leave?
  • Tell me about one holiday you remember better than all the others.

The people and the big questions

These are for later in the conversation, once the stories are flowing. Some families are happy to go here on a first visit, and some never will. Both are fine.

  • Who was the person you looked up to most as a child?
  • What did your parents teach you that you still believe?
  • What is something about your childhood that would surprise me?
  • What do you miss most about those years, and what are you glad has gone?
  • If you could spend one more ordinary afternoon back then, where would it be?

That is thirty-six questions, more than enough for a season of visits. You will not need them all. Two or three good ones, and the time to listen, are enough for an afternoon.

Frequently asked questions

What are good questions to ask grandparents about their childhood?

Small, specific ones about things and places: what was on the kitchen table, how they got to school, what they bought at the corner store, what happened on a snow day. Specific questions bring back scenes, and scenes become stories.

How do you start the conversation?

Start with an object, a photograph or a place, and ask about one particular day rather than a whole decade. A puzzle, a photo album or a short story read aloud can give you both something to talk about.

Should I record the conversation?

Ask first. Many people are happy to be recorded on a phone once they know it is for the family. If they would rather not, write down what you remember that evening.

What if they do not remember?

Move to a smaller question or a different subject. There are no wrong answers, and the conversation matters more than any single detail.
