The Story of Silent Night: One Christmas Eve in Oberndorf, 1818

The story of Silent Night: a priest's 1816 poem, a melody finished on Christmas Eve 1818, a guitar, and the broken-organ legend that came later.

Inkpot Lane · 10 November 2026 · 8 min read

On 24 December 1818, in Oberndorf, a village on the river Salzach in what is now Austria, a young assistant priest handed a poem to the schoolteacher from the next village. He had written it two years earlier, and he wanted music for it that day. By the end of the night the two of them had sung it in church for the first time. The story of Silent Night is usually told with a broken organ and hungry mice in it. The record tells a smaller story, and a better one.

The short answer: Joseph Mohr wrote the words of "Stille Nacht" in 1816, in the mountain parish of Mariapfarr. On 24 December 1818, in Oberndorf, he asked Franz Xaver Gruber to set them for two voices, a choir and a guitar. Gruber wrote the melody that day, and it was sung at the church of St. Nicholas that night. The popular tale of a last-minute organ failure is unsupported: sources describe the instrument as in poor repair for years, and no surviving evidence shows it suddenly failed that day. Traveling singers then carried the song across Europe and, by 1839, to New York.

The story of Silent Night begins with a poem, not a crisis

Joseph Mohr was an assistant priest at Mariapfarr, his father's home town in the Lungau region of Salzburg, when he wrote a six-verse German poem beginning "Stille Nacht! Heilige Nacht!" That was in 1816. The surviving record does not say what Mohr did with the poem over the next two years; Gruber's melody would not come until Christmas Eve 1818.

In 1817 Mohr became assistant priest at the newly established parish of St. Nicholas in Oberndorf, and it was there he met Franz Xaver Gruber. Gruber was the schoolteacher in nearby Arnsdorf, and since 1816 he had also been the organist at St. Nicholas. The two men had music in common, and Mohr played the guitar.

One Christmas Eve: 24 December 1818

What happened next comes to us mainly from Gruber himself. Many years later, on 30 December 1854, he wrote a short account of how the song began, because people in Berlin had asked. In that letter he says Mohr brought him the poem on Christmas Eve 1818 and asked for a melody for two solo voices with a choir, accompanied by a guitar. Gruber wrote it that evening, and that night, at the Christmas service in St. Nicholas, the two men sang it for the first time with the choir, to Mohr's guitar. Gruber later called it "a simple composition."

That is nearly all we know about the first performance. Gruber's account, written thirty-six years later, says the song met with general approval from those present; no independent report from 1818 survives, and nothing suggests anyone that night imagined how far it would travel.

What about the broken organ?

One familiar explanation for the guitar is that the church organ broke down just before Christmas, perhaps because mice had chewed through its bellows, leaving a desperate priest to find music that could be sung without it.

It is a good story, and it is a legend. The Hymnology Archive's history of the carol notes that other sources describe the Oberndorf organ as being in poor condition for a long time, so its being unavailable "was not a surprise", and it records the hymnologist Carl Daw's view that the broken-organ crisis and the mice belong with the details to discard. The organ was replaced in 1825. Gruber's own account asks for a guitar setting because that is what Mohr asked for. Flooding on the Salzach may well have made the old instrument worse; nothing in the record says it failed on the day.

The distinction matters. The legend turns the song into an emergency answer to a broken organ. The record tells something quieter: Mohr had written the poem two years earlier, and on Christmas Eve 1818 he asked Gruber for music with a guitar accompaniment. Why he chose the guitar is not recorded.

How a village song left the village

The next part of the story belongs to people who were not in the church that night.

According to Gruber's account, an organ builder who came to work on the Oberndorf instrument, Karl Mauracher, took a copy of the song home with him to the Zillertal valley in Tyrol. The Zillertal had something Oberndorf did not: families of traveling singers who toured Europe with Tyrolean songs. Two of them matter here.

The Strasser family, traveling glove makers who were also singers, are reported to have sung it in Leipzig in December 1832. The next year it appeared in print for the first time, in a small Dresden collection titled Four Genuine Tyrolean Songs, with no composer or poet named. To its first printed readers, it was a folk song from the mountains.

The Rainer family, another Zillertal group, took it further. They were touring the United States in 1839, and they are credited with the first American performance, in New York, that Christmas.

The song that lost its authors

By the 1840s "Stille Nacht" was well known in northern Germany, and the melody was slowly changing as it traveled. Its origins were drifting too. In Berlin some believed the tune was an old folk melody, others that it was by Michael Haydn, the brother of the composer Joseph Haydn. Over the years it was also credited to Mozart and to Schubert.

That confusion is why Gruber wrote his 1854 account at all: an inquiry came from Berlin, from the court of King Frederick William IV of Prussia, and he answered it. Mohr had died in 1848, in the Alpine village of Wagrain, and could not answer for himself.

Even then the question did not entirely settle. The firmest evidence came later: a manuscript of the song in Mohr's own hand, dated by researchers to about 1820-1825, credits the melody to Gruber. It was found in 1995 and is held by the Salzburg Museum.

Three verses in English

English-speaking churches mostly know the song through one translation. John Freeman Young, then serving at Trinity Church in New York, published it in 1859 in a small collection called Carols for Christmas Tide. It begins "Silent night, holy night", and it does not translate all six of Mohr's verses: Young took three of them, from the first, the sixth and the second German verses, in that order. That is why the English carol is shorter than the German one, and why a German speaker and an English speaker, singing side by side, are not always singing the same verse.

The Austrian Commission for UNESCO, which added the song to Austria's national inventory of intangible cultural heritage in 2011, says it has been translated into some 300 languages. The St. Nicholas church in Oberndorf is gone, lost to repeated flooding; a small memorial chapel, the Silent Night Chapel, stands near the place.

Why the small story is the better one

Strip away the mice, and what is left is two working men in a river town, one with a poem and one with a melody to write, making something for that night's service. The documented trail then runs through an organ builder, a family of glove makers who sang, and another family of traveling performers, each carrying the song a little farther. What began as music for one Christmas Eve reached people its makers could never have known.

If you sing it this Christmas Eve, that is the story worth telling the person next to you.

Frequently asked questions

Who wrote Silent Night?

Joseph Mohr, an assistant priest, wrote the words in German in 1816. Franz Xaver Gruber, a schoolmaster and church organist, wrote the melody on 24 December 1818. The English words most people sing are John Freeman Young's 1859 translation.

Was the organ really broken on the first Christmas Eve?

That is a legend. The sources describe the Oberndorf organ as in poor condition for years, not as failing that week, and the mice story has no documentary support. Gruber's own account says Mohr asked for a guitar setting.

Where was Silent Night first sung?

At the church of St. Nicholas in Oberndorf, near Salzburg, on Christmas Eve 1818. The church was later lost to flooding; the Silent Night Chapel now stands near the site.

Why does the English version have only three verses?

John Freeman Young translated three of Mohr's six German verses for his 1859 version, and that shorter carol is the one most English hymnals and carol books print.
