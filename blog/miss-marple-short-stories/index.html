Miss Marple Short Stories: All 20, in the Order They Appeared

Miss Marple short stories in order: all 20 of Agatha Christie's, from the Tuesday Night Club in 1927 to Greenshaw's Folly in 1956, and which books collect them.

Inkpot Lane · 11 November 2026 · 8 min read

In December 1927 readers of The Royal Magazine met a small gathering in a village sitting room. Six people agree to meet each week and take turns telling a real mystery whose answer only the teller knows; the others try to solve it. The quiet elderly lady whose house they meet in is the one who solves it. That story, "The Tuesday Night Club", was the first appearance of Agatha Christie's Miss Marple, and it began a run of Miss Marple short stories that lasted almost thirty years.

The short answer: Christie wrote 20 Miss Marple short stories (alongside 12 novels), according to Wikipedia's article on the character. Thirteen are collected in The Thirteen Problems (1932). The other seven are in the current edition of Miss Marple's Final Cases: six from the original 1979 collection, plus "Greenshaw's Folly" (1956). All twenty are also gathered in one volume, Miss Marple: The Complete Short Stories. The full list, in order of first appearance, is below.

Miss Marple is a character created by Agatha Christie; this is an independent reading guide, not connected with the Christie estate. We give titles and dates only, and no solutions.

Why read the Miss Marple short stories?

The short stories show Miss Marple's method in a compact form. Wikipedia's article on the character describes it as resting on shrewd intelligence and long observation of village life in St Mary Mead. In the Tuesday Night Club stories, that method works within a single evening's puzzle, and you can watch it work from first fact to last.

They also suit the way many of us read now: one story at a sitting, with time to stop and guess before the end.

The Thirteen Problems (1932): the first thirteen

The Thirteen Problems was published in the UK by Collins Crime Club on 18 June 1932, and in the US by Dodd, Mead in 1933 under the title The Tuesday Club Murders. It has two groups of six stories told at gatherings, followed by a final story, "Death by Drowning", set outside those gatherings.

The first six: the Tuesday Night Club. The club meets in Miss Marple's own house. The members, as Wikipedia lists them, are her nephew Raymond West, a well-known author; the artist Joyce Lemprière; Sir Henry Clithering, a former commissioner of Scotland Yard; Dr Pender, a clergyman; and Mr Petherick, a solicitor. Each tells a mystery whose answer they already know. These six ran in The Royal Magazine from December 1927 to May 1928:

  1. "The Tuesday Night Club" (December 1927)
  2. "The Idol House of Astarte" (January 1928)
  3. "Ingots of Gold" (February 1928)
  4. "The Blood-Stained Pavement" (March 1928)
  5. "Motive v. Opportunity" (April 1928)
  6. "The Thumb Mark of St. Peter" (May 1928)

The next six: a dinner party at the Bantrys'. Later, Sir Henry and Miss Marple are guests at a dinner party at the home of Colonel and Mrs Bantry, and the game is played again with new tellers. These six appeared in magazines between December 1929 and May 1930. Five first appeared in Britain's The Story-Teller Magazine; "The Four Suspects" first appeared in the American Pictorial Review. In order of first appearance (the book prints them in a different order):

  1. "The Blue Geranium" (December 1929, The Story-Teller)
  2. "A Christmas Tragedy" (January 1930, The Story-Teller, first printed as "The Hat and the Alibi")
  3. "The Four Suspects" (January 1930, Pictorial Review, US; April 1930 in The Story-Teller)
  4. "The Companion" (February 1930, The Story-Teller, first printed as "The Resurrection of Amy Durrant")
  5. "The Herb of Death" (March 1930, The Story-Teller)
  6. "The Affair at the Bungalow" (May 1930, The Story-Teller)

Stories 8 and 9 both appeared in January 1930; we have not found which reached readers first.

The last one: 13. "Death by Drowning", first published in Nash's Pall Mall Magazine in November 1931.

The other seven: Miss Marple's Final Cases

The first collection called Miss Marple's Final Cases and Two Other Stories was published by Collins Crime Club on 17 October 1979. As the title says, it contained eight stories, two of them without Miss Marple. The Agatha Christie website now lists the collection with nine stories, seven of them featuring Miss Marple, because "Greenshaw's Folly" has been added. In order of first publication, the seven are:

  1. "Miss Marple Tells a Story": first broadcast on BBC radio on 11 May 1934; first printed in 1935 as "Behind Closed Doors".
  2. "Strange Jest": This Week (US), 2 November 1941.
  3. "Tape-Measure Murder": This Week (US), 16 November 1941.
  4. "The Case of the Caretaker": The Strand Magazine, January 1942.
  5. "The Case of the Perfect Maid": The Strand Magazine, April 1942, first printed as "The Perfect Maid".
  6. "Sanctuary": Woman's Journal, October 1954, written for the Westminster Abbey restoration appeal.
  7. "Greenshaw's Folly": first published in the UK in the Daily Mail, 3-7 December 1956, and first collected in The Adventure of the Christmas Pudding in 1960.

Several of these stories also appeared in earlier collections, under different groupings in Britain and the US, which is why lists online sometimes disagree. If you want all twenty in one book, look for Miss Marple: The Complete Short Stories, which the Agatha Christie website lists with all twenty. Alternatively, The Thirteen Problems plus an edition of Miss Marple's Final Cases that includes "Greenshaw's Folly" will cover them. Check the contents before buying, because not every edition of Final Cases includes that story.

What order should you read them in?

  • Start with The Thirteen Problems, in book order. The first six introduce the Tuesday Night Club, so they read best together and in order.
  • Then the later seven, in any order. They do not depend on each other, so pick by mood or by length.
  • Read each one as a puzzle. The Tuesday Club format practically asks you to: every story is told aloud by someone who knows the answer, so stop when the teller stops and make your own guess before Miss Marple gives hers.

Why the format still works

The Tuesday Night Club is an old device, a circle of people trading puzzles, and it works because it is a game the reader can join. Everyone hears the same facts, everyone is free to reason from them, and the listener who solves it is the one who noticed which ordinary detail mattered. That is the shape of a fair whodunit, and if you want to know where the word and the rules came from, our article What is a whodunit? tells that story.

Frequently asked questions

How many Miss Marple short stories are there?

Twenty, according to Wikipedia's article on the character: thirteen in The Thirteen Problems and seven more, now gathered in the current edition of Miss Marple's Final Cases. All twenty are in Miss Marple: The Complete Short Stories.

What was the first Miss Marple story?

"The Tuesday Night Club", published in The Royal Magazine in December 1927. It became the first chapter of The Thirteen Problems in 1932, and it came out before her first novel, The Murder at the Vicarage (1930).

What is the Tuesday Night Club?

The circle of six people in The Thirteen Problems who meet at Miss Marple's house and take turns telling real mysteries whose answers only the teller knows. The others try to solve each one; Miss Marple does.

Are The Thirteen Problems and The Tuesday Club Murders the same book?

Yes. The Tuesday Club Murders is the title the collection was given in the United States in 1933.

Which collection should I buy to read them all?

Miss Marple: The Complete Short Stories collects all twenty in one volume. You can also read them across The Thirteen Problems and an edition of Miss Marple's Final Cases that includes "Greenshaw's Folly". Check the contents before buying.
