Why Do Snowflakes Have Six Sides? What Snowflake Bentley Saw

Why do snowflakes have six sides? The way water molecules stack as they freeze, and the Vermont farmer who first photographed a snow crystal in 1885.

Inkpot Lane · 14 November 2026 · 9 min read

On 15 January 1885, on his family's farm near Jericho, Vermont, a nineteen-year-old named Wilson Bentley pointed a camera through a microscope at a single snow crystal and made the first photograph of one. He had been trying for about two years. Over the next forty-six winters he photographed more than five thousand of them, and he became known as "Snowflake" Bentley. Almost every picture he took raises the question children still ask at a frosty window: why do snowflakes have six sides? Bentley could show the six arms more clearly than anyone before him. The reason for them lies in the way water freezes.

The short answer: A snow crystal has six sides because ice is a hexagonal crystal. As water vapor freezes, its molecules lock into a lattice that repeats in a six-sided pattern, and the crystal grows outward from that pattern. The six arms look alike because they grow at the same moment, through the same cloud, at the same temperature and humidity. Most real snow crystals are less tidy than the famous ones, and no two follow exactly the same path through the sky.

A microscope, a black tray and a feather

Bentley's interest started early. The Old Farmer's Almanac tells how his mother gave him an old microscope when he was fifteen, and how the first thing that caught him was a snowflake. Two years later he asked for a better microscope and a camera. His father thought that fussing with snowflakes was a waste of time, but he gave in.

The method Bentley worked out was patient and cold. He caught falling snow on a wooden tray painted black, so the crystals would show up. Wearing big mittens, he lifted a single crystal with a broom straw onto a glass slide, sometimes nudging it into place with a feather, and he was careful not to breathe on it. The Snowflake Bentley exhibit in Jericho quotes what he saw, in his own words of 1925: "Every crystal was a masterpiece of design and no one design was ever repeated."

His life's work came out in November 1931 as a book called Snow Crystals, written with W. J. Humphreys and filled with more than two thousand of his photographs. Bentley died at the family farmhouse on 23 December 1931, of pneumonia, a few weeks after it was published.

Why six? Kepler asked first

Bentley was not the first person to wonder. In 1611 the astronomer Johannes Kepler wrote a short essay, The Six-Cornered Snowflake, and gave it to the Holy Roman Emperor Rudolf II as a New Year's gift. He was the first scientist to try to explain why snow crystals have six corners. He guessed that the answer had something to do with the way round things pack together, the way cannonballs stack in a pile, but the science that could test that idea did not exist yet. In 1637 René Descartes published the first detailed description of snow crystals, made with the naked eye, and was the first to recognize the capped column, a shape that comes up again below.

The real reason: how water molecules stack

The answer Kepler was groping toward is in the ice itself. A water molecule is one oxygen atom with two hydrogen atoms, and when water freezes, the molecules link to their neighbors in a fixed arrangement. Kenneth Libbrecht, the Caltech physicist whose site snowcrystals.com is a standard reference on the subject, puts it simply: the six-fold symmetry of a snow crystal comes from the way water molecules line up in the ice crystal lattice, in a precise hexagonal array.

That six-sided pattern is tiny, far too small to see, but it repeats over and over. A snow crystal starts as a speck of ice in a cloud and grows as water vapor freezes onto it. Because the lattice underneath is hexagonal, the simplest crystal it can make is a flat or long six-sided prism, and every larger shape grows outward from those six faces.

Why the six arms match

Here is the part that puzzles people most. If each arm grows on its own, how does it "know" what the other five are doing? According to Libbrecht, it doesn't. There is no hidden signal between the arms. They match because they share a history. All six grow at the same time, through the same pocket of air, so when the crystal drifts into a colder or damper patch of cloud, all six arms feel the change at once and respond in the same way.

That also explains why so many real snow crystals are lopsided. Libbrecht notes that the great majority are not very symmetrical at all. A collision, an uneven gust or a crystal tumbling as it falls is enough to spoil the pattern. The perfect six-armed stars on greeting cards are the lucky ones, and they are also the ones Bentley chose to photograph.

Plates, stars or needles: the temperature decides

Not every snow crystal is a star. The shape depends mostly on the temperature of the cloud where it grows, and on how much water vapor is in the air.

  • Around -15 C (5 F): the large, branched stars, the kind on Christmas cards, grow in a narrow band of temperature around this point, along with thin, flat plates.
  • Around -6 C (21 F): crystals grow long instead of wide, as needles and columns.
  • More moisture, more branches: in damp air the arms sprout side branches; in drier air the crystals stay as simple six-sided prisms.
  • A change on the way down: when a crystal falls from one temperature into another, it can change shape partway, which is how a column can end up with a flat plate at each end, like a tiny spool: the capped column.

The first person to grow snow crystals on purpose, to test all this, was the Japanese physicist Ukichiro Nakaya. He succeeded in his laboratory in 1936, growing the world's first synthetic snow crystals on a single rabbit hair. Libbrecht is careful to say that exactly why the crystals switch between plates and columns is still not fully understood.

So, are no two snowflakes alike?

Bentley believed it: no one design, he said, was ever repeated. Libbrecht's answer is more precise: because no two snow crystals follow exactly the same path through the clouds as they fall, no two look exactly alike. A large, branched star records thousands of small changes in its journey, and the chance of two making the identical trip is too small to worry about.

See it for yourself on the next snowy day

On a cold day, a good magnifying glass can help you see the shape of larger snow crystals. If you want to photograph their fine details with a phone, try a macro mode or a clip-on macro lens rather than relying on ordinary digital zoom.

  1. Use a dark surface, as Bentley did. A dark mitten, a scarf or a piece of dark card works.
  2. Cool it first. Leave it outside for a few minutes so the crystals land without melting.
  3. Catch a few and look quickly. Do it in a light, steady fall rather than a heavy wet one, and try not to breathe on them.
  4. Count the arms. Look for six, and then look for the ones that are broken, lopsided or shaped like tiny columns. Those are just as true a record of the cloud.

Frequently asked questions

Why do snowflakes have six sides?

Because ice is a hexagonal crystal. When water vapor freezes, the water molecules lock into a lattice that repeats in a six-sided pattern, and a snow crystal grows outward from the six faces of that pattern.

Are all snowflakes six-sided?

Nearly all snow crystals are built on a six-sided pattern, but many do not look like stars. Some are long columns or needles, some are simple plates, and many are broken or lopsided from collisions on the way down.

Who took the first photograph of a snowflake?

Wilson A. Bentley, a farmer near Jericho, Vermont, on 15 January 1885. He went on to photograph more than five thousand snow crystals.

Is it true that no two snowflakes are alike?

In practice, yes: each crystal records its own path through the clouds, and no two paths are exactly the same, so no two crystals look exactly alike.
