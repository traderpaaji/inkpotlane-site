Charlotte Mason Copywork: What She Actually Wrote About It

Charlotte Mason copywork, in her own words from Home Education: short lessons, one perfect line, favorite verses, and the eyes-shut habit for spelling.

Inkpot Lane · 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

Search for Charlotte Mason copywork and you will find plans, notebooks and passage lists. Then open the book she wrote for parents, Home Education, and look for the word "copywork". In the online edition of volume 1 that we searched, it does not appear once. The section is called something plainer: Transcription. The section is short, is in the public domain, and gives specific advice about choosing passages, spelling and lesson length.

The short answer: In Home Education (first published 1886), Charlotte Mason called it transcription. For children of seven or eight, she wanted slow, beautiful copying of passages the child chose, in lessons of no more than ten minutes to a quarter of an hour, treated as an introduction to spelling: look at a word, see it with your eyes shut, then write it from memory. Dictation of a prepared passage came later, from about eight or nine.

Who Charlotte Mason was, briefly

Charlotte Mason (1842-1923) was a British educator. According to Wikipedia, she gave a series of lectures on the education of children under nine, later published as Home Education in 1886, and in 1891 she moved to Ambleside, in England, where she established the House of Education. The quotations below come from the full text of volume 1 published online by AmblesideOnline, with page numbers from that edition. Mason revised her books over the years, and this edition mentions a handwriting book from 1898, so it is a later text than the very first 1886 printing.

What she wrote, line by line

1. It begins with handwriting, not with passages

Before the Transcription section comes a section on Writing, and it sets the tone for everything after. Her first instruction is: "let the child accomplish something perfectly in every lesson—a stroke, a pothook, a letter." And: "Let the writing lesson be short; it should not last more than five or ten minutes." (pp. 233-234)

The goal of a lesson is not a page. She says the lesson is "a single line which is as exactly as possible a copy of the characters set," and that the child may have to write several lines before producing it (p. 235).

2. Transcription comes first, before dictation

"The earliest practice in writing proper for children of seven or eight should be, not letter writing or dictation, but transcription, slow and beautiful work" (p. 238). Notice the age: seven or eight, in this passage. She places transcription before prepared dictation, but that does not mean she wanted children to avoid all writing of their own until they had mastered both.

3. It is a way into spelling

Her instruction on the connection between transcription and spelling is direct: "Transcription should be an introduction to spelling. Children should be encouraged to look at the word, see a picture of it with their eyes shut, and then write from memory." (p. 238)

So for Mason the point was never only neat letters. The child copies word by word, but is encouraged to hold each word in the mind's eye first, and then write it without looking.

4. The child chooses the lines

"A certain sense of possession and delight may be added to this exercise if children are allowed to choose for transcription their favourite verse in one poem and another." She adds that this is better than copying a whole poem, "an exercise which stales on the little people before it is finished", and suggests "a book of their own, made up of their own chosen verses." (p. 238)

5. Short lessons, carefully set up

"Not more than ten minutes or a quarter of an hour should be given to the early writing-lessons. If they are longer the children get tired and slovenly." She asks for double-ruled lines and "small text-hand" at first, because children tend to rush into very tiny writing that is hard to undo (pp. 238-239). She also writes about posture and light; that advice reflects the classrooms and pens of her day.

6. Then dictation, prepared in advance

In the next section she is fierce about dictation "as commonly practised", calling it perhaps "the most mischievous" of school exercises, because children see and copy their own misspellings. Her version is different: "A child of eight or nine prepares a paragraph, older children a page, or two or three pages," studying the hard words with eyes shut before anything is read aloud. Only then is the passage dictated, clause by clause (pp. 240-242).

Her words next to common modern habits

Here is a side-by-side comparison you can use to check a modern copywork routine against Mason's text.

What Home Education saysA habit to check in your own routine
Ages seven or eight for transcriptionStarting long copying with much younger children
Ten minutes to a quarter of an hourCopying a full page because the page has room for one
One line copied as exactly as possibleMeasuring a lesson by how many lines are filled
The child chooses favorite versesEvery passage chosen by the adult
Look, see it with eyes shut, write from memoryCopying letter by letter while glancing up for each one
Dictation only after preparationDictating unseen passages and marking errors in red

None of these is a rule you must follow. They are simply what she wrote, which is worth knowing before you use her name.

A Charlotte Mason copywork session in ten minutes

  1. Let the child choose a favorite line from a poem, as Mason suggested, or a line from a story they like. Begin with a line they can copy carefully in the time you have.
  2. Read it together and talk for a moment about anything interesting in it: a long word, a comma, a name.
  3. Look and picture. Ask the child to look at one tricky word, close their eyes, and see it. Then open them and check.
  4. Copy slowly, on lined paper with a clear midline, aiming for one line done as well as they can, not as many as they can.
  5. Stop before the work becomes rushed. Aim for about ten minutes rather than filling a page. The child can keep a carefully copied line in a book of their own chosen passages.

Frequently asked questions

What did Charlotte Mason call copywork?

In Home Education she called it transcription. The word "copywork" does not appear in the online text of volume 1 that we searched.

At what age did Charlotte Mason start copywork?

She wrote that the earliest writing practice "for children of seven or eight" should be transcription. Before that, her writing lessons were about forming single strokes and letters well.

How long is a Charlotte Mason copywork lesson?

Short. She wrote that early writing lessons should take "not more than ten minutes or a quarter of an hour," because longer ones make children tired and careless.

What should children copy in Charlotte Mason copywork?

She suggested letting children choose their favorite verse from one poem and another, and keeping them in a book of their own chosen verses.

What is the difference between copywork and dictation for Charlotte Mason?

Transcription (copywork) came first: copying a passage slowly and well. Prepared dictation came later, from about eight or nine: the child studies a paragraph, especially its hard words, and then writes it as it is read aloud.
