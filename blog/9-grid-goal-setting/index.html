Ohtani Goal Chart: What His 9x9 Grid Said, and What Came True

Shohei Ohtani filled an ohtani goal chart with 64 small actions before the draft. What it said, the goal that missed, and how to make your own.

Inkpot Lane · 10 September 2026 · 8 min read

At Hanamaki Higashi High School in Iwate, Japan, in the winter of his first year (2010-11), a sixteen-year-old pitcher filled in a sheet of paper divided into 81 squares. In the middle square he wrote the biggest thing he could imagine: to be picked in the first round of the professional draft by eight teams. Out at the edges, among 64 much smaller squares, he wrote things like pick up the trash and clean my room. The student was Shohei Ohtani, and the sheet is now widely known as the ohtani goal chart.

The short answer: The ohtani goal chart is a square of 81 boxes: one big goal in the center, eight supporting goals around it, and eight small actions for each support, 64 in all. It comes from Japan, where versions of the layout are known as the Mandala Chart, Mandal-Art and, for goal-setting specifically, Open Window 64. It works by turning a wish into things you can do this week.

What happened to that chart, including the part that did not come true, is a better lesson in goal-setting than most books on the subject.

The chart a high-school pitcher filled in

Ohtani's coach at Hanamaki Higashi, Hiroshi Sasaki, had every player fill in a goal sheet like this one. Who first taught it is disputed. The Harada Institute says Ohtani used the Harada Method there, learned by Sasaki from the institute itself (2017; on the Wall Street Journal, 2018). Sasaki's own account, excerpted on nippon.com, says the sheet was his own design, built from corporate training systems and business books. Neither rules out the other, and no source settles it. Players lived on campus and went home about six days a year, per Wikipedia.

Around his center goal, Ohtani placed eight supports: building his body, control, sharpness, speed of 160 km/h, breaking balls, luck, character and mental strength (Sports Nippon, first published 2 February 2013, via draft-kaigi.jp). Four of the eight are pitching skills — control, sharpness, breaking balls and the 160 km/h speed target — and one is his body. The other three, luck, character and mental strength, get the same space on the page as his fastball.

And the actions around luck are the ones people remember: picking up litter, cleaning his room, his attitude toward umpires, reading books, being someone people support, and greetings. A teenager who wanted to be a top draft pick decided that picking up litter belonged in the plan, next to fastball speed. Superstition or good manners, it was small enough to do that afternoon.

The goal that did not come true

Here is the part usually left out. In October 2012, four days before the Japanese professional draft, Ohtani announced that he wanted to skip Japan's league and go straight from high school to Major League Baseball in the United States (2012 NPB draft). Most teams stayed away, wary of spending a pick on a player who might never sign. On draft day, October 25, 2012, exactly one team chose him in the first round: the Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters.

Not eight teams. One. The Fighters won him over with a pitch about minor-league life abroad and the chance to both pitch and hit. He signed, played five seasons in Japan, and joined the Angels in 2018. By early 2026 the Harvard Gazette was counting two World Series titles and four MVP awards.

Meanwhile, one support came true exactly. One square said, in effect, speed 160 km/h — and on 19 July 2012, in an Iwate prefecture semifinal, he threw exactly that (99 mph), the first Japanese high-schooler ever recorded at the mark (Number).

The lesson: the center is a direction, the squares are the work

It would be easy to tell this as "write down your dream and it will happen." That is not what happened. The exact goal in the center square missed, partly because of a choice Ohtani made himself. What did happen was bigger than the goal he wrote, and it grew out of the other squares.

Three lessons are worth taking from that:

  • Write the center goal big, but hold it loosely. It points you in a direction, not a contract with the future.
  • Spend your effort on what you control. Ohtani could not control eight draft rooms. He could control whether he trained, practiced and picked up the trash.
  • Plan for the parts of you that are not skills. Mind, character and luck earned their own blocks, because goals usually fail for reasons other than a missing skill.

One honest caveat: nobody can prove the chart made him the player he became — talent, coaching and years of work cannot be pulled apart. What it did was keep the plan visible.

Where the ohtani goal chart comes from

The grid is older than Ohtani's chart, and its history is more tangled than most pages let on.

The nine-square layout was developed in 1979 by the Japanese consultant Yasuo Matsumura, who called his tool the Mandala Chart (Mandala Chart Association; Japanese Wikipedia). In English creativity books it usually appears as the Lotus Blossom technique, credited to "Yasuo Matsumura of Clover Management Research in Chiba City" (Michalko). In 1987 the designer Hiroaki Imaizumi published his own version, Mandal-Art, in a book about note-taking for creativity (hanmoto); Japanese Wikipedia says it built on Matsumura's method, but as an idea-generation tool, not a goal-setting one.

Takashi Harada is a separate figure again: a former Osaka junior-high teacher whose track and field athletes won 13 individual national titles in seven years (Japanese Wikipedia). He built a 9x9 goal grid of his own, Open Window 64, one tool of his wider Harada Method, and has said the grid alone is not enough — he pairs it with a written long-term goal sheet (President Online). No source ties Open Window 64's origin to either Matsumura's or Imaizumi's chart; the honest description is a mandala-style layout Harada built for goal-setting.

Three names, three purposes: the Mandala Chart and Lotus Blossom for creative thinking, Mandal-Art for generating ideas, Open Window 64 for goals. Ohtani's chart follows the goal-setting shape.

How to build your own 9x9 goal grid

You need one sheet of paper and about an hour. Draw a square, divide it into nine blocks, then divide each block into nine squares: 81 in all.

Step 1: Write one big goal you could prove

Write it so a stranger could check whether you did it. "Get fit" cannot be proved. "Run 5 km without stopping by September 30" can. Choose just one. If you are stuck, a wheel of life assessment or an ikigai exercise will help.

Step 2: Choose eight supports, not eight skills

Write eight things that must be true for the goal to happen, and don't stop at skills — take the hint from Ohtani's ring. A useful starting set: skill, body, money, mindset, habits, people, tools or place, and rest. Cross out what doesn't fit, and merge duplicates.

Step 3: Give each support eight small actions

Each support moves to the middle of its own outer block and gets eight actions around it. Keep every action between five and twenty minutes, and write behaviors, not results. "Walk 15 minutes after dinner" is an action; "be fit" is a result, and you cannot do a result.

A worked example

Say the big goal is to open a small online shop, and one support is learn product photos. Its eight actions might be:

  • Watch one 10-minute lighting video
  • Photograph one product from five angles
  • Edit one photo start to finish
  • Copy the style of a shop you admire
  • Clear and light the table top
  • Ask one person which photo is better
  • Write down your three camera settings
  • Reshoot last week's worst photo

None of these is hard, and any could be done tonight — the same logic as picking up the trash: small, specific, done today.

Step 4: Tick, repeat, and look at it every week

Tick a square the first time you do it; some actions are one-offs, others worth repeating all year — and keep looking at it. Most homemade charts fail right here, filled in with care in January and folded into a drawer by February.

Frequently asked questions

What was on Shohei Ohtani's goal chart?

In the center: to be a first-round draft pick for eight professional teams. Around it, eight supports: building his body, control, sharpness, speed of 160 km/h, breaking balls, luck, character and mental strength — each with eight small actions, such as picking up litter.

Did Ohtani achieve the goal on his chart?

Not the center goal. Only the Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters picked him in the first round, after he said he wanted to go straight to Major League Baseball. One support came true exactly: a 160 km/h fastball on 19 July 2012, a first for a Japanese high-schooler.

How small should the actions be?

Five to twenty minutes each, written as behaviors rather than results. Split anything longer in two.
