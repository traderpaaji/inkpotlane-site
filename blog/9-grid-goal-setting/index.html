9 Grid Goal Chart: The Lesson in Ohtani's High-School Plan

As a teenager, Shohei Ohtani filled a 9 grid goal chart with 64 small actions. What it teaches, including the goal that missed, and how to make your own.

Inkpot Lane · 10 September 2026 · 7 min read

At Hanamaki Higashi High School in Iwate, in northern Japan, a young pitcher filled in a sheet of paper divided into 81 squares. In the middle square he wrote the biggest thing he could imagine: to be picked in the first round of the professional draft by eight teams. Out at the edges, among 64 much smaller squares, he wrote things like pick up the trash and clean my room. The student was Shohei Ohtani, and the sheet was a 9 grid goal chart.

The short answer: A 9 grid goal chart is a square of 81 boxes: one big goal in the center, eight supporting goals around it, and eight small actions for each support, 64 in all. It comes from Japan, where it is called Mandal-Art or a mandala chart, and it works by turning a wish into things you can do this week.

What happened to that chart, including the part that did not come true, is a better lesson in goal-setting than most books on the subject.

The chart a high-school pitcher filled in

Ohtani's coach at Hanamaki Higashi, Hiroshi Sasaki, had studied the goal-setting method of Takashi Harada and had his players use it, according to a Wall Street Journal story summarized by the Lean Blog. The players lived on campus and went home only a few days a year (Wikipedia), so baseball there was not a hobby. It was the whole day.

Around his center goal, Ohtani placed eight things that would have to be true for it to happen. In the English translation printed by the Harvard Gazette, they read: physical conditioning, mental strength, control, sharpness, speed, trickery (his breaking balls), character and karma, or luck.

Five of the eight are about pitching. The other three are not: mental strength, character and luck get the same space as fastball speed.

And the actions around luck are the ones people remember: pick up the trash, clean his room, show respect toward umpires, be a person that people root for. A teenager who wanted to be a top draft pick decided that picking up litter belonged in the plan. Superstition or good manners, it was small enough to do that afternoon.

The goal that did not come true

Here is the part of the story that usually gets left out.

In October 2012, four days before the Japanese professional draft, Ohtani announced that he wanted to skip Japan's league and go straight from high school to Major League Baseball in the United States (2012 NPB draft). Most teams, not wanting to waste a first-round pick on a player who might never sign, stayed away. On draft day, October 25, 2012, exactly one team chose him in the first round: the Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters.

Not eight teams. One.

The Fighters made their case with a presentation on the hard life of young players in the American minor leagues, and the chance to both pitch and hit. He signed with them, played five seasons in Japan, and joined the Los Angeles Angels in 2018. By early 2026 the Harvard Gazette was counting two World Series titles and four MVP awards.

Meanwhile, one of his supports came true almost to the number. In the summer of 2012 he threw a 160 km/h (99 mph) fastball, at the time a Japanese high-school record.

The lesson: the center is a direction, the squares are the work

It would be easy to tell this as "write down your dream and it will happen." That is not what happened. The exact goal in the center square missed, partly because of a choice Ohtani made himself. What did happen was bigger than the goal he wrote, and it grew out of the other squares: the body, the speed, the small daily things.

Three lessons are worth taking from that:

  • Write the center goal big, but hold it loosely. It points you in a direction. It is not a contract with the future.
  • Spend your effort on what you control. Ohtani could not control how eight draft rooms would behave. He could control whether he trained, practiced and picked up the trash.
  • Plan for the parts of you that are not skills. Mind, character and luck earned their own blocks, because goals usually fail for reasons other than a missing skill.

One honest caveat: nobody can prove the chart made him the player he became. Talent, coaching and years of work cannot be pulled apart. What the chart did was keep the plan visible, in squares small enough to act on.

Where the 9 grid goal chart comes from

The grid is older than Ohtani's chart. The Japanese designer Hiroaki Imaizumi developed a nine-by-nine thinking chart he called Mandal-Art, usually dated to 1979, its name a nod to the Buddhist mandala. Takashi Harada, a teacher and coach, later built a 64-action goal version of it into his Harada Method, which has been used in Japanese schools, sports teams and companies. You will also see it called a mandala chart, a lotus chart, a 9x9 grid or simply a goal map.

How to make your own 9 grid goal chart

You need one sheet of paper and about an hour. Draw a square, divide it into nine blocks, and divide each block into nine squares: 81 in all.

Step 1: Write one big goal you could prove

Write it so that a stranger could check whether you did it. "Get fit" cannot be proved. "Run 5 km without stopping by September 30" can. Choose just one. If you are stuck, a wheel of life assessment or an ikigai exercise will help you choose.

Step 2: Choose eight supports, not eight skills

Around the center, write eight things that must be true for the goal to happen. Take the hint from Ohtani's ring and don't stop at skills. A useful starting set: skill, body, money, mindset, habits, people, tools or place, and rest. Cross out any that don't fit, and merge any two that say the same thing.

Step 3: Give each support eight small actions

Each support moves to the middle of its own outer block and gets eight actions around it. Keep every action between five and twenty minutes, and write behaviors rather than results. "Walk 15 minutes after dinner" is an action. "Be fit" is a result, and you cannot do a result.

A worked example

Say the big goal is to open a small online shop, and one support is learn product photos. Its eight actions might be:

  • Watch one 10-minute lighting video
  • Photograph one product from five angles
  • Edit one photo start to finish
  • Copy the style of a shop you admire
  • Clear and light the table top
  • Ask one person which photo is better
  • Write down your three camera settings
  • Reshoot last week's worst photo

None of these is hard, and any of them could be done tonight. It is the same logic as picking up the trash: small, specific, and done today.

Step 4: Tick, repeat, and look at it every week

Tick a square the first time you do it; some actions are one-offs, others worth repeating all year. And keep looking at it. Most homemade charts fail right here: filled in with care in January, folded into a drawer by February.

Frequently asked questions

What was on Shohei Ohtani's goal chart?

In the center: to be a first-round draft pick for eight professional teams. Around it, eight supports: physical conditioning, mental strength, control, sharpness, speed, breaking balls, character and luck, each with eight small actions, such as picking up trash.

Did Ohtani achieve the goal on his chart?

Not exactly. Only one team, the Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters, picked him in the first round of the 2012 draft, after he said he wanted to go straight to Major League Baseball. His career went far beyond it, but the center square did not come true as written.

What is a 9 grid goal chart?

A goal-setting chart of 81 squares: one big goal in the center, eight support goals around it, and eight small actions for each support, 64 in all. It is also called a mandala chart, Mandal-Art or a 9x9 grid.

How small should the actions be?

Between five and twenty minutes each, written as behaviors rather than results. If an action would take longer than twenty minutes, split it in two.
