How to Make a Logic Puzzle: Build a Christmas Whodunit in 5 Steps

How to make a logic puzzle with one fair answer: choose the culprit, build a suspect table, write clues, then test for one solution and spare clues.

Inkpot Lane · 10 November 2026 · 8 min read

If someone at your Christmas table always finishes the cracker puzzle before the paper hats are on, this year you could make a puzzle for them instead of buying one: a small whodunit, written on a single card, that they solve at the table between dinner and dessert. Learning how to make a logic puzzle is easier than it looks, and the rule that makes it fair fits on one line. We will build one together below, from an empty page to a tested puzzle, and show the mistake that nearly broke it.

The short answer: Decide the answer first. Give every suspect the same few facts, set out in a table (a register), and make the list complete. Write clues that are all true of the answer and together rule out everyone else. Then run two tests: all the clues together must leave exactly one suspect (that proves a unique answer), and, if you want a tight puzzle, removing any single clue should let someone else back in (that proves every clue is needed). Finally, hand it to someone who has not seen it.

What a logic puzzle needs, and what it must not have

A logic puzzle is fair when everything needed to solve it is on the page, and nothing has to be guessed. That gives you two jobs as the setter:

  • Enough. The clues, read exactly, must lead to one answer and only one. If two suspects survive, the solver has to guess.
  • No unnecessary clues, if that is your aim. A clue can knock out suspects along the way and still not be essential to the final answer. For a compact card puzzle like ours, we aim to make every clue necessary. That is a design choice, not a law: a puzzle with a spare clue can still be fair and have one answer.

The second job is the easy one to miss. We missed it too, in the first draft below.

How to make a logic puzzle, step by step

Step 1: Choose the answer before anything else

It feels backwards, but it is the simplest way to build a fair puzzle. Our puzzle is "Who took the last mince pie?" at a village carol practice, and the answer is Cora. Everything else is built around her.

Step 2: Build the register

A register is a table: one row for each possible suspect, and the same few facts for everyone. Three facts are plenty for a first puzzle. Ours are what each person wore, the time they arrived, and what they carried in. Eight suspects fit on a card. Say the ground rules on the card too: everyone who could have taken the pie is on the list; each person wore the scarf shown all through practice; the arrival times and what each person carried in are accurate; and every clue is true. Without a complete list and reliable clues, ruling out seven names would not prove the eighth.

Notice that Cora's red scarf is shared by Agnes and Gwen, and her basket by Bert, Dev and Hal. Her arrival time, 3:40, is her own, but no clue simply names it. That is deliberate: if one clue pointed straight at an unusual fact, it would give the game away. The solver should have to combine clues.

Step 3: Write clues that each cut the list

Now write clues that are true of Cora and, together, rule out every other suspect. Our first draft had four:

  1. The taker was not wearing blue.
  2. The taker arrived after 3:20.
  3. The taker carried a basket.
  4. The taker was not wearing green.

All four are true of Cora. Together they leave only her. It looks finished.

Step 4: Run the two tests

Test one: do all the clues together leave one suspect? Yes. Not blue removes Dev and Frank; after 3:20 removes Agnes and Hal; a basket removes Elsie and Gwen; not green removes Bert. Cora is left.

Test two: take away each clue in turn. Does the puzzle break? This is the leave-one-out test, and it is where the draft failed. Take away clue 1, and Dev survives alongside Cora: clue 1 matters. Take away clue 3, and Gwen survives: it matters. Take away clue 4, and Bert survives: it matters. But take away clue 2, the time clue, and the answer is still Cora. Applied in order, clue 2 did rule out Agnes and Hal, but the other clues ruled them out anyway (Agnes carried a lantern, not a basket; Hal wore green). It was not essential.

The fix is to change a different clue so the time clue becomes necessary. We replaced clue 3 with "the taker did not arrive carrying an umbrella." Agnes, who arrived with a lantern, is now ruled out only by the time clue; remove it, and both Agnes and Cora are left.

The finished clues, with the count after each one:

  1. The taker was not wearing blue. Dev and Frank are out. Six left.
  2. The taker arrived after 3:20. Agnes (3:10) and Hal (3:05) are out. Four left.
  3. The taker did not arrive carrying an umbrella. Gwen is out. Three left: Bert, Cora, Elsie.
  4. The taker was not wearing green. Bert and Elsie are out. Cora took the pie.

And the leave-one-out test now passes: without clue 1, Dev survives; without clue 2, Agnes; without clue 3, Gwen; without clue 4, Bert and Elsie. Every clue is needed, and together they leave one name. The register lets you check this yourself, row by row.

Step 5: Read every clue exactly, then hand it over

Before a stranger sees it, read each clue for loopholes. "After 3:20" does not include 3:20 itself, but a solver should never have to wonder: if the register records times to the minute, "3:21 or later" makes the boundary plain. Puzzle books often settle this kind of question with a rule printed at the front. Make sure no clue depends on knowledge outside the card, such as which scarf color is "Christmassy."

Then give it to someone who has not watched you make it, without explaining anything. If they ask a question, check whether the card already answers it. If a fact or rule is genuinely missing, add it and run both tests again.

Making it harder, or easier

  • Easier: fewer suspects (five or six), and clues that each remove two or three.
  • Harder: clues that make the solver check two columns together, such as "the taker arrived after 3:30 and brought a basket." Combining facts makes a clue more demanding, though more rows or conditions do not automatically make a better puzzle.
  • Longer: keep a checkpoint halfway ("after clue 3, three suspects remain"), so a solver who slips can find the slip.

A whole Christmas built this way

Our worked example is a small version of the format behind The Twelve Cases of Christmas. In that casebook the snow closes the road into the made-up town of Tinselby on 19 December, and twelve small mysteries follow, one after another, to Christmas Eve. Each case has its own register of suspects and clues that rule people out until one name is left, from 40 suspects in the first case to 1,500 in the last, 5,550 in all, with no name used twice. It opens with pages on how to solve a case and on reading a clue exactly ("before 4:30" means 4:29 or earlier), gives every case two checkpoints, and lists in the solutions how many suspects should remain after each clue. Case 1 takes about ten minutes. It is cozy crime, gentle in tone, and a 6 x 9 inch paperback for a pencil and a ruler.

Frequently asked questions

How do you make a logic puzzle with only one answer?

Choose the answer first, then write clues that are all true of it, and make sure the suspect list is complete. Check that the clues together rule out every other suspect. If you also want every clue to be essential, remove them one at a time and check that each removal lets another suspect back in.

How many clues does a logic puzzle need?

There is no fixed number: you need enough to establish one answer. In our eight-suspect example four clues are enough, and we deliberately made each one necessary. Other puzzles add extra clues to guide the solver.

What is a leave-one-out test?

Remove one clue and solve with the rest. If the same single answer remains, that clue is not needed for a unique answer. It is a useful editing test for a tight elimination puzzle, though a spare clue does not make a puzzle unfair.

How do I make a logic puzzle harder?

Add suspects and facts, and write clues that combine two facts at once. Keep checkpoints so a solver can confirm progress partway through.
