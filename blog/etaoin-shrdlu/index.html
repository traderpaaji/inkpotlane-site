Etaoin Shrdlu: The Typesetter's Mistake That Maps English

Etaoin shrdlu is the nonsense that slipped into old newspapers from Linotype keyboards. Why it happened, and how its letter order helps you solve cryptograms.

Inkpot Lane · 11 November 2026 · 7 min read

Every so often, a reader of a newspaper set in hot metal would come across a line of type that made no sense at all: etaoin shrdlu, sometimes repeated, sitting in the middle of a column as if the paper had briefly lost its mind. In its ordinary printing-shop use it was neither a code nor a joke: it was a typesetter's way of marking a line to be thrown away, and it happens to spell out, almost exactly, the most common letters of the English language. Etaoin shrdlu is a little piece of printing history, and it is also one of the most useful things a cryptogram solver can know.

The short answer: Etaoin shrdlu is the sequence of letters in the first two columns of keys on a Linotype typesetting machine, which were arranged roughly by how often each letter is used in English. When an operator made an error, it was quicker to finish the line by running a finger down those keys and throw the line away; sometimes the throwaway line was printed by accident. The letters E, T, A, O, I, N, S, H, R, D, L, U are, approximately, the twelve most common in English.

The machine that cast a line at a time

The Linotype was invented by Ottmar Mergenthaler, and the first one in commercial use was installed in the printing office of the New York Tribune in July 1886. Instead of setting each letter by hand, the operator typed at a keyboard and the machine cast a whole line of text as a single piece of metal, called a slug.

The keyboard was not arranged like a typewriter's. According to Wikipedia's article on the machine, the letters were placed roughly by frequency, with the most used on the left. The lowercase keys in the first column read e, t, a, o, i, n; the second column read s, h, r, d, l, u; and the rest of the alphabet followed in the order c, m, f, w, y, p, then v, b, g, k, q, j, then x, z.

Why the nonsense got into the paper

Correcting a wrongly assembled line was cumbersome, and once a line had been cast as a solid metal slug, individual letters could not simply be replaced. So when an operator noticed an error partway through a line, the fastest thing to do was to fill out the rest of the line with anything at all, let the machine cast it, and throw it away. Running a finger down the first two columns of keys produced etaoin shrdlu. The nonsense was also a signal to the proofreader that this line was to be removed.

Most of the time it was. Occasionally, the throwaway slug slipped through and was printed, mostly in newspapers, where speed mattered most. The phrase became so familiar that it took on a life of its own: Wikipedia lists three pieces in The New Yorker in 1925 under the pen name "Etain Shrdlu", a 1942 story by Fredric Brown called "Etaoin Shrdlu", and a 1972 computer program named SHRDLU. When The New York Times set its last edition in hot metal, on 2 July 1978, the documentary about that night was called Farewell, Etaoin Shrdlu. Newspapers had largely replaced the machines with phototypesetting by the 1970s and 1980s.

The letters, by the numbers

Why did those twelve letters end up together? The Linotype keyboard used an arrangement based on how frequently letters appear in English, which Wikipedia's letter-frequency article traces to the experience and custom of compositors who set type by hand. That order is older than modern counts of large bodies of text, and those counts show a close match, although the precise order varies with the texts being counted.

By that count, the order from most to least common is E, T, A, O, I, N, S, H, R, D, L, C, U, M, W, F, G, Y, P, B, V, K, J, X, Q, Z. The first eleven match etaoin shrdl; then C slips in ahead of U. Other counts differ a little: Wikipedia's article on letter frequency gives several orders, including Herbert Zim's "ETAON RISHD LFCMU GYPWB VKJXZQ", and notes that letter frequencies vary by writer and by subject. E ranks first in the general-English tables discussed here, but the exact order varies, particularly farther down the list; Zim's first twelve, for example, include F rather than C or U. The twelve Linotype letters are a useful approximation, not a fixed ranking for every kind of English text.

How etaoin shrdlu helps with a cryptogram

In a simple substitution cryptogram, each letter of a message is consistently replaced by another letter, and the solver must work out the swaps. If the message were long enough, the letter that appears most often would very likely stand for E, the next for T, and so on down the etaoin shrdlu list. Edgar Allan Poe used exactly this idea in his 1843 story "The Gold-Bug", and the method itself goes back much further: Wikipedia credits the Arab scholar al-Kindi, in the ninth century, with formally developing frequency analysis to break ciphers.

But a puzzle-book cryptogram is short, often one sentence of a few dozen letters, and in a sample that small the counts wobble. The most common code letter might be T or A or S rather than E. So use the list as a set of suspects, not an answer key:

  1. Count the code letters and mark the three or four most frequent.
  2. Treat them as likely members of the top dozen, E, T, A, O, I, N, S, H, R, D, L, U, rather than assuming the top one is E.
  3. Test against word shapes. A three-letter word that starts with a frequent letter is a good candidate for THE; a single letter on its own is almost always A or I.
  4. Leave uncertain letters open. A code letter that appears only once gives you little frequency evidence. It might stand for an uncommon letter, but it could also be a common one that happens to appear only once in this short message. Let the words around it decide.

Frequently asked questions

What does etaoin shrdlu mean?

Nothing, as words. It is the order of the first two columns of lowercase keys on a Linotype keyboard, which typesetters used to fill out a line they meant to throw away. The letters are roughly the twelve most common in English.

Why did etaoin shrdlu appear in newspapers?

When a Linotype operator made a mistake, it was quicker to fill out the line with these keys and discard the cast line than to fix it. Now and then the discarded line was printed by accident.

What are the most common letters in English?

E is the most common, followed (in Lewand's count) by T, A, O, I, N, S, H, R, D, L, C and U. Different counts vary a little, but E, T and A are always near the top.

Does etaoin shrdlu help solve cryptograms?

Yes, as a starting list of likely letters. In a short cryptogram, though, the counts are unreliable, so combine it with word shapes such as THE, AND and single-letter words.

What is the least common letter in English?

In Lewand's table, Z, at about 0.07 percent of letters, just behind Q and X.
