Old Christmas Hymns: How Old Is Each One, Really?

Old Christmas hymns have three ages: the words, the tune and the English we sing. Dated sources for six, from a Spanish poet of about 400 to a 1599 German hymn.

Inkpot Lane · 12 November 2026 · 6 min read

One of the old Christmas hymns still sung today began around the year 400 with a man in northern Spain who had spent his career in law and government and then turned to a life of poverty and privacy, writing Christian poems. His name was Aurelius Prudentius, and he is thought to have lived from about 348 to 413. One of his poems, a long "Hymn for All Hours," praises Christ from creation to the end of time. Shortened to a few verses, it has been sung at Christmas for centuries and is still sung today, as "Of the Father's Love Begotten." That makes its words among the oldest you can still find in a Christmas hymnal. It also shows why "how old is it?" is a harder question than it looks. Every hymn has three ages at once.

The short answer: For any old Christmas hymn, ask three questions: how old are the words, how old is the tune, and how old is the English version we sing? "Of the Father's Love Begotten" has words from about 400, a tune printed in 1582 and an English text of the 1850s. "Let All Mortal Flesh Keep Silence" rests on a liturgy of the late 300s or early 400s, a French folk tune printed in 1860 and English words of 1864. "O Come, O Come, Emmanuel" grows from chants of about the 700s, but its tune was traced to a 15th-century manuscript only in 1966.

The three ages

A hymn is a stack of layers, and each layer has its own date.

  • The words. The oldest layer is usually Latin, Greek or German, and it may be older than anyone can date exactly.
  • The tune. It often has nothing to do with the words it now carries. Some tunes began as spring songs, folk songs or garland dances.
  • The English. Most of what English-speaking congregations sing was put into English by Victorian translators, mostly in the 1850s and 1860s.

Notice how often the tune is the stranger one. "Good King Wenceslas" is a winter carol on a spring tune. "Let All Mortal Flesh" is a very ancient prayer paired with a French folk tune from Picardy, first printed in 1860 after it was collected from a woman who remembered it from childhood; how old the tune really is, nobody knows. And the haunting tune of "O Come, O Come, Emmanuel," the one that now sounds so medieval, was unsourced until 1966, when a scholar found it in a 15th-century French manuscript under a different text.

Not as old as you think

Some Christmas songs sound medieval and are not. The Hymnology Archive's page on "The First Nowell" says the oldest known copy is a manuscript that John Hutchens made for Davies Gilbert around 1816-17, and the earliest printing is Gilbert's second edition of 1823. The word "nowell" is much older, since Chaucer used it around 1395, but that dates the word, not the carol.

The same rule applies in the other direction. Words can be much older than they feel. Prudentius wrote his hymn when the Roman Empire still stood, and a congregation singing it today is singing something about sixteen hundred years old, in a nineteenth-century English dress.

How to date a hymn yourself

  1. Find the oldest source named for the words. A hymnal's footnote that says "Latin, 4th century" is a claim; look for the manuscript or the first printing.
  2. Check whether the tune was written for the words. If it was borrowed, find its first appearance separately.
  3. Date the English translation. A translator's year is usually the easiest of the three to find, and it is the one that actually reached your church.
  4. Ask what the source says about the earliest copy, not the earliest mention. A name in an old list proves a title, not a text.

Frequently asked questions

What is the oldest Christmas hymn?

It depends on how you count. "Of the Father's Love Begotten" draws on a poem by Prudentius, who lived about 348-413, and "Let All Mortal Flesh Keep Silence" rests on a liturgy whose Orthodox form dates to the late 4th or early 5th century. Neither has a single founding date.

Why do old hymns have such different tunes?

Words and tunes were often paired much later. "Good King Wenceslas" uses a spring song from 1582, and "Let All Mortal Flesh" uses a French folk tune first printed in 1860.

Who translated the old Latin hymns into English?

Mostly Victorian clergymen and scholars. John Mason Neale translated several, including "Of the Father's Love Begotten" and "O Come, O Come, Emmanuel." Catherine Winkworth translated the German ones.

Are old Christmas hymns still under copyright?

The old words themselves are not, but a modern translation or arrangement can be. That is why a book of hymns should name the printing it uses.
