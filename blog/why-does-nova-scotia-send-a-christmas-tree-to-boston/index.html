Why Does Nova Scotia Send a Christmas Tree to Boston?

Why does Nova Scotia send a Christmas tree to Boston? A thank-you for a relief train that left Boston 13 hours after the 1917 Halifax Explosion.

Inkpot Lane · 14 November 2026 · 8 min read

On the morning of 6 December 1917, a telegram reached a Boston investment firm from its man in Halifax, Nova Scotia. It asked for a relief train, doctors, nurses and supplies, and it ended: "Not time to explain details but list of casualties is enormous." About an hour earlier, soon after nine o'clock, a ship loaded with explosives for the war in Europe had blown up in Halifax harbor after a collision. By eleven o'clock that morning the governor of Massachusetts had wired Halifax that his state stood ready "to go the limit." That night a train left Boston for the north. If you have ever asked, "Why does Nova Scotia send a Christmas tree to Boston every year?" that train is the answer.

The short answer: Nova Scotia sends Boston a Christmas tree each year to say thank you for the help Boston and Massachusetts gave after the Halifax Explosion of 6 December 1917. A relief train of doctors, nurses and supplies left Boston that same night and fought through a blizzard to reach the city. Halifax is reported to have sent a thank-you tree in 1918, and the yearly gift began in 1971. The tree stands on Boston Common as the city's official Christmas tree, and in 2026 it will be lit on Thursday, 3 December.

The worst morning in Halifax's history

Halifax was a busy wartime port in 1917, its harbor full of ships carrying soldiers and supplies. On the morning of 6 December, the French ship Mont-Blanc, loaded with explosives, collided with the Belgian relief ship Imo in the narrow part of the harbor. The collision started a fire on the Mont-Blanc, and after burning for almost twenty minutes the fire set off her cargo, soon after 9 a.m. The Canadian War Museum calls the result the largest man-made explosion before the atomic bombs.

The Canadian War Museum gives the official death toll as 1,963; other accounts give about 2,000 or almost 1,800. About 9,000 people were injured and about 6,000 were left homeless. Windows broke miles away. Then the weather turned, and a blizzard followed the next day.

Thirteen hours

Boston did not wait. The Massachusetts Historical Society records that news reached the city by telegraph about an hour after the explosion, and that by 11 a.m. Governor Samuel McCall had answered Halifax: "Massachusetts stands ready to go the limit in rendering every assistance you may be in need of."

The man put in charge of the relief train was a Boston businessman, Abraham C. Ratshesky. In a matter of hours he and the state's public safety committee gathered surgeons from the State Guard, nurses, Red Cross workers and medical supplies. The Society's account puts the rest plainly: "At ten o'clock that night the relief train left Boston." That was about thirteen hours after the explosion.

Through the snow

The blizzard that hit Halifax on 7 December hit the railroad too. The train was slowed by deep snow on the way north, and it did not reach Halifax until the morning of 8 December. When it arrived, Ratshesky's party was the first medical relief party to reach the city from the United States, and its doctors and nurses went straight to work.

Help kept coming after that. On 8 December, the people of Boston packed Faneuil Hall for a mass meeting about the relief, with the governor and the mayor among the speakers. Money was raised by public subscription across the state, and people collected clothing and other necessities. Legion Magazine gives the final total of the Massachusetts-Halifax Relief Committee's donations as $716,000. Other accounts count the aid in other ways, so the figures you will find vary.

Why one thank-you became a yearly gift

Halifax did not forget. Legion Magazine and NBC Boston both report that in 1918 the city sent Boston a Christmas tree as a token of thanks. We have not seen a record of that first tree from the time itself, so treat the date as the one most often told.

What is certain is the modern tradition. According to Legion Magazine, in 1971 the Lunenburg County Christmas Tree Producers Association began giving Boston a tree every year, partly to promote Nova Scotia's Christmas tree exports and partly to remember 1917, and the provincial government later took the gift over. NBC Boston also dates the yearly tree to 1971. The Canadian War Museum describes it as "a token of friendship for the aid Bostonians rendered in December 1917."

How the tree is chosen

The tree is not picked at random. The Nova Scotia government asks anyone who knows of a suitable tree to send a photo to the Department of Natural Resources and Renewables. Its published checklist is short:

  • The kind: a white spruce, a red spruce or a balsam fir.
  • The height: twelve to fifteen meters, about 40 to 50 feet.
  • The look: healthy, with good color, medium to heavy density, and uniform and symmetrical in shape.
  • The place: easy to reach, so it can be cut and loaded safely.

Some years the tree has come from a family's own land. The 2024 tree, for example, was a 45-foot white spruce from Mattie Settlement, in Antigonish County, according to NBC Boston. Once cut, the tree travels south by road, and it is set up on Boston Common, where it stays through the holiday season as the city's official Christmas tree.

Watching the lighting

The tree is lit at a ceremony on Boston Common, traditionally on the first Thursday after American Thanksgiving. For 2026 the Nova Scotia government lists the lighting for Thursday, 3 December, from 6 to 8 p.m., with the ceremony shown on WCVB-TV and online for anyone who cannot be on the Common.

It is worth telling the story to whoever is watching with you. The lights go on in a park full of strangers, for a gift from people they will never meet, to thank a city for a train that left at ten o'clock on a winter night more than a hundred years ago.

Frequently asked questions

Why does Nova Scotia send a Christmas tree to Boston?

To thank Boston and Massachusetts for their help after the Halifax Explosion of 6 December 1917, when a relief train of doctors, nurses and supplies left Boston the same night and reached Halifax through a blizzard.

When did Nova Scotia start sending the tree?

Halifax is reported to have sent a thank-you tree in 1918. The yearly gift began in 1971 and has continued since.

What kind of tree is it?

A white spruce, red spruce or balsam fir, about 40 to 50 feet tall, chosen for its health, color and symmetrical shape.

When is the Boston Common tree lit in 2026?

The Nova Scotia government lists the lighting for Thursday, 3 December 2026, from 6 to 8 p.m. on Boston Common, shown on WCVB-TV and online.
