Is Coloring for Kids Just Busywork? The Myth and the Evidence

Is coloring for kids just busywork? The idea goes back to a 1947 textbook. What the evidence says, where the critics are right, and a better way to color.

Inkpot Lane · 10 September 2026 · 5 min read

Picture a rainy Saturday. Your five-year-old has been bent over a picture for twenty minutes, tongue poking out, deciding whether the elephant should be purple. Then someone at the table says it, kindly enough: "It's just busywork, though, isn't it?" The idea that coloring for kids is mindless filler sounds like common sense. It actually has a birthday, 1947, and an author.

The short answer: Coloring won't teach reading or math on its own, and it shouldn't replace free drawing. But "busywork" undersells it. Finishing a picture practices hand control, focus and calm, and the case against coloring books is thinner than its reputation.

The myth, stated fairly

The fair version goes like this. A coloring book hands a child someone else's picture, so the child makes no real decisions. Filling in shapes keeps small hands busy, which is handy for the adult, but the child learns little and may lose some of their own ideas along the way. Better, the argument goes, to give them a blank sheet.

That is a serious position, held by serious people. It is also only half right, and the story of where it came from shows which half.

Where the busywork idea came from: a professor and a bird

In 1947 Viktor Lowenfeld, an Austrian-born art educator teaching at Penn State, published Creative and Mental Growth. It became one of the most influential textbooks in art education, and many teacher-training programs used it.

On coloring books, Lowenfeld was blunt: "There is general agreement that coloring books too are detrimental to children's creative expression."

To show why, his book included an illustration about birds. Asked to draw a bird on a blank sheet, children drew all kinds of inventive birds. After coloring workbook birds drawn as a simple curved V, many of them began drawing their own birds the same flat way.

He also doubted that coloring in workbook balloons and kites taught children to count or read, and he suggested, a little wickedly, that one reason teachers used workbooks was to get a rest. That is the busywork charge in its original form, and it has been repeated ever since.

What the evidence actually says

Here is the surprise: the famous claim rested on an argument and an illustration, not on a controlled experiment. Forty-four years later, a researcher was still looking for the evidence behind it. In 1991 I. L. King published a paper in Studies in Art Education with a telling title: "In Search of Lowenfeld's Proof That Coloring Books Are Harmful to Children."

When a 2016 public radio report revisited the debate, the case against coloring books still rested on Lowenfeld's reasoning and on the views of art educators, not on experiments with children.

The research on coloring's upside is real, but narrower than many articles suggest. In a 2005 study in the journal Art Therapy, Curry and Kasser gave 84 college students a short task designed to make them anxious, then had them color a mandala, a plaid pattern, or a blank sheet of paper. Anxiety fell about equally for the mandala and plaid groups, and by more than it did for free coloring on blank paper. Structured coloring, in other words, was calming.

Two honest cautions. Those were college students, not five-year-olds, and one study is not a law of nature. Anyone who promises that coloring will boost a child's brain by some percentage is going well past what the research shows.

What coloring for kids actually does

You don't need statistics for this part. You can see it at your own table:

  • It trains the hand. Gripping a crayon, pressing hard enough for color and steering around a curve use the same small muscles a child needs to hold a pencil.
  • It stretches attention. A picture has an end. Getting there is practice at staying with one thing.
  • It calms. Quiet, repetitive and absorbing: the same quality the Curry and Kasser study found in adults.
  • It still holds decisions. The outline is given. The colors, the order and the green sun are not.
  • It opens conversation. Sitting side by side, with nobody staring at anybody, children talk.

Where the critics are right

Lowenfeld's worry about copying deserves respect. A child who only ever colors other people's outlines misses the harder, braver work of putting their own marks on an empty page. And coloring a picture of five kites does not, by itself, teach anyone to count to five.

So the answer isn't coloring or drawing. It's coloring and drawing, with talk in between.

A better way to color: story, talk, then draw

  1. Start with a story. A picture that means something holds a child longer than one that just fills time. Tell the story first, then hand over the crayons. Our Ganesh Chaturthi story for kids and the Night Before Christmas poem are both written to be read aloud.
  2. Talk about the picture, not the neatness. "Why do you think he's smiling?" beats "stay inside the lines."
  3. Leave the colors alone. A purple elephant is a decision, not a mistake.
  4. Keep a blank sheet beside the book. When the picture is finished, ask them to draw what happens next. That is the direct answer to Lowenfeld's bird.
  5. Choose pictures they can finish. Thick lines and big shapes suit ages 4 to 8; finer detail suits older children. And protect the page underneath: our guide to markers that don't bleed through has the fixes.

Frequently asked questions

Is coloring good for kids?

Yes, in modest and useful ways. It builds the hand control needed for writing, stretches attention, and gives children a calm, quiet activity to share with a grown-up. It works best alongside free drawing, not instead of it.

Do coloring books kill creativity?

The idea comes from Viktor Lowenfeld's 1947 textbook, which rested on argument rather than controlled experiments. Pairing coloring with free drawing, and letting children choose their own colors, answers the concern.

At what age should kids start coloring?

Many children start scribbling with chunky crayons at around two. Simple pictures with thick lines and big shapes usually work from about three.

Is coloring good for handwriting?

It helps. Coloring uses the same grip and small hand muscles as writing, so it is a playful way to build the strength and control children need before they write letters.
