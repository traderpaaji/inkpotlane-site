The Night Before Christmas Poem: Full Text & Who Wrote It

Read the night before Christmas poem online in full, then see who wrote it, how it first appeared in 1823, and why its authorship is still disputed.

Inkpot Lane · 10 September 2026 · 8 min read

On 23 December 1823, readers of the Troy Sentinel, a newspaper in upstate New York, turned a page and found 56 lines of verse with no name attached. The editor printed them anyway, with a note admitting he had no idea whom to thank. Those lines became the night before Christmas poem, and two hundred years later people are still arguing about who wrote it.

The short answer: It was first printed anonymously, as "Account of a Visit from St. Nicholas." Clement Clarke Moore, a New York professor, claimed it in 1844. The family of Henry Livingston Jr. has long said he wrote it, and nobody has settled it. The full text is just below.

The full text of the night before Christmas poem

Here it is in full, couplet by couplet, as first printed. One couplet is set apart in gray italics; the story of who wrote it, and why that couplet matters, follows the poem.

’Twas the night before Christmas, when all through the house
Not a creature was stirring, not even a mouse;

The stockings were hung by the chimney with care,
In hopes that St. Nicholas soon would be there;

The children were nestled all snug in their beds,
While visions of sugar-plums danced in their heads;

And mamma in her 'kerchief, and I in my cap,
Had just settled our brains for a long winter's nap,

When out on the lawn there arose such a clatter,
I sprang from the bed to see what was the matter.

Away to the window I flew like a flash,
Tore open the shutters and threw up the sash.

The moon on the breast of the new-fallen snow
Gave the lustre of mid-day to objects below,

When, what to my wondering eyes should appear,
But a miniature sleigh, and eight tiny reindeer,

With a little old driver so lively and quick,
I knew in a moment it must be St. Nick.

More rapid than eagles his coursers they came,
And he whistled, and shouted, and called them by name:

"Now, Dasher! now, Dancer! now Prancer and Vixen!
On, Comet! on, Cupid! on, Donder and Blitzen!"

"To the top of the porch! to the top of the wall!
Now dash away! dash away! dash away all!"

As dry leaves that before the wild hurricane fly,
When they meet with an obstacle, mount to the sky;

So up to the house-top the coursers they flew,
With the sleigh full of toys, and St. Nicholas too.

And then, in a twinkling, I heard on the roof
The prancing and pawing of each little hoof.

As I drew in my head, and was turning around,
Down the chimney St. Nicholas came with a bound.

He was dressed all in fur, from his head to his foot,
And his clothes were all tarnished with ashes and soot;

A bundle of toys he had flung on his back,
And he looked like a peddler just opening his pack.

His eyes, how they twinkled! His dimples, how merry!
His cheeks were like roses, his nose like a cherry!

His droll little mouth was drawn up like a bow,
And the beard of his chin was as white as the snow;

The stump of a pipe he held tight in his teeth,
And the smoke it encircled his head like a wreath;
(the one couplet the coloring book leaves out)

He had a broad face and a little round belly,
That shook when he laughed, like a bowlful of jelly.

He was chubby and plump, a right jolly old elf,
And I laughed when I saw him, in spite of myself;

A wink of his eye and a twist of his head,
Soon gave me to know I had nothing to dread;

He spoke not a word, but went straight to his work,
And filled all the stockings; then turned with a jerk,

And laying his finger aside of his nose,
And giving a nod, up the chimney he rose;

He sprang to his sleigh, to his team gave a whistle,
And away they all flew like the down of a thistle.

But I heard him exclaim, ere he drove out of sight,
"Happy Christmas to all, and to all a good-night!"

A poem that arrived with no name

The story most often told is that Harriet Butler, a Troy minister's daughter, had a copy of the poem and showed it to the Sentinel's editor, Orville L. Holley.

Holley ran it as "Account of a Visit from St. Nicholas," with a note that began: "We know not to whom we are indebted for the following description of that unwearied patron of children."

The professor who claimed it

Clement Clarke Moore taught at the General Theological Seminary in New York City, and a comic poem about a flying sleigh was not the reputation he was after. His name was first attached to it in print in 1837, in The New-York Book of Poetry. In 1844 he put it in his own collection, Poems, saying he had written it "not for publication, but to amuse my children."

For more than a century, that settled it.

The family who said it was theirs

Henry Livingston Jr. (1748–1828) was a New Yorker with Dutch roots on his mother's side, a former army major, and distantly related to Moore's wife. His descendants grew up with a family story: the poem was really his.

The trouble is the paper trail. No record survives of Livingston claiming the poem himself, and no printing with his name on it has ever been found. He died in 1828, sixteen years before Moore's book.

The detectives take sides

In 2000 the literary scholar Don Foster made the case for Livingston. The rhythm and word habits, he argued, matched Livingston's other writing; the Dutch touches suited a man with a Dutch mother; and Moore, who he said disliked tobacco, would never have given St. Nicholas a pipe.

Seth Kaller, a dealer in historical documents, answered point by point. He cited a Moore poem, "The Wine Drinker," that hardly reads like the work of a strict abstainer, and noted that Moore's friend Washington Irving had already given St. Nicholas a pipe in his 1809 History of New York.

The computers split too. A 2016 statistical study of style by MacDonald P. Jackson favored Livingston; two computer analyses in 2023 favored Moore. Four copies survive in Moore's handwriting, but a handwritten copy proves only that somebody wrote the words out, not who wrote them first.

What the argument can't change

Here is the turn in the story, and it has nothing to do with the author. Before this poem, Americans pictured St. Nicholas in all sorts of ways. After it, the picture settled: plump and jolly, dressed in fur, eight tiny reindeer, down the chimney, stockings filled, a finger laid aside his nose. Much of the Santa Claus people draw today started in these lines.

And whoever wrote it, it was written to be heard: four strong beats to a line, rhymes like a drumbeat, and a last line shouted into the night. It has lasted two centuries because families kept reading it aloud.

The reindeer names in 1823: Dunder and Blixem

Read the poem closely and you'll notice the last two reindeer don't quite sound like the two you know. In the 1823 printing, Dasher, Dancer, Prancer and Vixen are named exactly as they are today, and so are Comet and Cupid. But the final pair are called Dunder and Blixem, close to Dutch words for thunder and lightning. That Dutch flavor is one of the details Don Foster later pointed to when he argued the poem was Henry Livingston's, whose mother was Dutch.

The names didn't stay put. By the time Clement Clarke Moore included the poem in his own 1844 collection, the pair had become Donder and Blitzen, with Blitzen borrowed from German rather than Dutch, and rhyming more neatly with Vixen and Comet. Later printings drifted further still, to the "Donner and Blitzen" most people say today. Whichever version you read aloud, all eight names still call the reindeer to fly, and that's the part that hasn't changed in two centuries.

How to read it aloud to a young child

  • Go slower than you think. The rhythm carries you, so you can linger on the good parts.
  • Let them shout the reindeer. Pause before "Now, Dasher!" and let them call the names with you.
  • Explain one old word, not ten. Make it "coursers": fast horses, and here, the reindeer.
  • Give them something to look at. Old-fashioned words are much easier to follow with a picture for each couplet.

Frequently asked questions

Who wrote The Night Before Christmas?

It was first printed anonymously in 1823. Clement Clarke Moore was named as the author in 1837 and published it among his poems in 1844. Descendants of Henry Livingston Jr. say he wrote it, and scholars still disagree.

What is the real title of the poem?

It was first printed as "Account of a Visit from St. Nicholas" and is often published as "A Visit from St. Nicholas." Most people know it by its first line, "'Twas the night before Christmas."

How long is the poem?

The original has 56 lines, or 28 couplets, and takes three to four minutes to read aloud.

Is The Night Before Christmas in the public domain?

Yes. It was published in 1823, so anyone may print, read and share the text.
